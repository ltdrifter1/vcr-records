Homework

Daft Punk’s debut, recorded at home in Paris and released in Europe on 20 January 1997, made a filter, a loop, and a joke into a worldwide house album.

Made at the house

Homework is the debut album by Daft Punk, Thomas Bangalter and Guy-Manuel de Homem-Christo. Virgin and Soma Quality Recordings released it in Europe on 20 January 1997. The United States followed on 25 March. They recorded it between 1994 and 1996 at Daft House, their home studio in Paris, which is where the title comes from: a record made at home, cheaply, quickly, and on purpose. It runs 73 minutes and 53 seconds. Nilesh Patel mastered it at the Exchange in London. The music is French house, techno, disco, and Chicago house, braided until the braid is the style.

The singles were already a career before the album existed. Da Funk came out on Soma on 8 May 1995, with Rollin’ & Scratchin’. Indo Silver Club followed on 26 February 1996, at first without a clear artist name on the sleeve. Around the World was released on 7 April 1997, Burnin’ on 15 September 1997, Revolution 909 on 16 February 1998. Bangalter has said they made so many tracks that an album appeared, and that they sequenced it to fill the four sides of a double vinyl LP. Virgin signed them in 1996 after the Soma singles started a bidding war. They kept their masters through Daft Trax. Virgin got the distribution. The control was the point.

Loops that are songs

The sound is repetition used as a hook, not as a failure of ideas. Around the World is one phrase, a disco bassline, a synthetic melody, and a filter that opens and closes like a curtain. Chris Power, at BBC Music, called that combination one of the decade’s catchiest singles: post-disco boogie, a little keyboard, a nagging line. Da Funk is the other immortal, a bouncy funk figure and an acid squelch, a sample of Vaughan Mason & Crew’s Bounce, Rock, Skate, Roll sitting under a riff that feels like a cartoon of Chic. It is funny and it is physical. Teachers is a roll call, a riff on a Dance Mania record that names the house producers the duo considered their faculty, Romanthony and Todd Edwards among them. Revolution 909 opens with a raid and then argues, in the only way a dance track can argue, by continuing. Funk Ad, at the end, is Da Funk backwards. The album bows, and winks.

Daftendirekt, the opener, is a piece of a live recording from the first I Love Techno in Ghent, on 10 November 1995. The home studio and the rave are both in the sequence. That double origin matters. Homework is not a bedroom record that hopes a club might one day notice. It is a club record made in a bedroom by two people who already knew what a room does to a loop. The filters are the arrangement. When a low-pass closes, the track inhales. When it opens, the track stands up. There are very few elements, and each one is loud in the mix on purpose.

France put the album at number 3 and kept it on the chart for 82 weeks. Britain put it at number 8. The Billboard 200 put it at number 150, which describes a different country’s radio and says little about the dance charts, where Da Funk and Around the World both reached number one. By February 2001 Virgin counted more than two million copies worldwide. They had pressed only 50,000 CDs at the start, because the duo wanted the run to be mostly vinyl. Demand corrected them. What Homework changed is the map. French house had been a Parisian and a specialist fact. This album made it a world music, and it made a home studio look like a reasonable place to invent a pop future. Five years from now the jokes will still be jokes, and the loops will still be songs. A filter opening on Around the World remains one of the cheapest, truest thrills in dance music. You do not need the helmets. You need the record.
