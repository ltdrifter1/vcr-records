Saved My Life

i! Records issued Todd Edwards' Saved My Life in the US in 1995, catalogue IR-117. He told a Red Bull Music Academy lecture in 2013 that an Ensoniq EPS held about 15 to 30 seconds, and that England sped the records up and called the result speed garage.

Discogs lists Todd Edwards’ Saved My Life as a one-sided American 12-inch, i! Records IR-117, in 1995, and a UK promo, FFRR and Internal FXDJ 279, dated 5 August 1995. The promo is four mixes: the original, Grant Nelson’s Ultimate Salvation remix, Joey Musaphia’s Kentian Soul Vibe, and DJ Sneak’s Flashback mix. At the Red Bull Music Academy in 2013, talking to Emma Warren, Edwards called “Saved My Life” a landmark, the track that put him on the map under his own name. He had made it after a night of hearing Little Louie Vega. Hardrive’s “Bass Tone” had the hard, repeating figure. Barbara Tucker’s “I Get Lifted” had the feeling. The night, he said, produced about two weeks of new tracks, and this was one of them. He then lost confidence in it. His manager at the time told him it was going out anyway.

The record is the useful object because he has also described the machine it had to fit inside. He had an old Ensoniq EPS. It held, he told Warren, about 15 to 30 seconds of sampling time. The quantise on it was what he wanted: a hard triplet shuffle. The other half of the idea came from records he named. Kenny “Dope” Gonzalez, in Masters at Work, programmed the shuffles, the 16th-note triplets. Marc Kinchen, MK, cut vocals up. Enya used a voice as a musical element rather than as a singer delivering a lyric, and Edwards asked why a sampled voice could not do that job instead of an organ or a piano. He put the three together. What he thought he had made was house with a shuffle. He allowed that it might simply have been sloppy house. His roots, he said, were in house. England sped the tempo up. The phrase that got attached was “speed garage,” and he did not hear it as praise. People wanted to step away from the phrase. He remembered a rift between house and UK garage. His own distinction, given in the lecture, was that house was smoother and less sample-heavy, and that garage, as he was using the word, was the shuffle, the swing, and the quantity of samples.

Thirty-three, played at forty-five

The early tracks could not be the dense ones. There was no room on the sampler. As the equipment grew he went, in his wording, crazy: more than a hundred samples in a track at times. A sample sitting more than three half-steps off the key of the track lost its quality. Pitch it five or six notes and, even with the formant pulled back, it fell apart. He kept the sources close to the key and chose them as a palette. Female vocals. An organ with a cymbal on top of it, which he liked for the scratch. The limiting case was a remix, not a theory. On St Germain’s “Alabama Blues” he had to borrow a friend’s Ensoniq EPS, because his own keyboard could not hold the music and the vocal at the same time. France, he told Warren, came before England. He was listening to DJ Disciple’s show, heard a French record, heard his own name among the producers being thanked, and realised it was St Germain. Two weeks later he was asked to remix a track. The borrowed machine is the footnote to that call. A vocal mix did not fit in the memory he already owned.

When the memory ran out mid-record he had a workaround, and he described it as part of the two or three weeks a track used to take. He built as he went. Drums down, then a pass through a record for a sound, sample it, try it, move on. If he liked something and the sampling time was gone, a 33 RPM record went on at 45, so the phrase occupied less space. Played back down at the track’s tempo, it left an artifact. Too low, and it distorted. The distortion was not a separate effect in the story he told. It was what the pitch did when the seconds had been saved. Later he changed the order of operations. He would sample 25 sounds onto the keys, save the bank, and do another, starting from about four banks, the ingredients set out before the track. The early way was the search conducted inside the limit. The later way was a library. Both of them treat the sample as material that has to be prepared, not as a loop dropped on a beat that is already finished.

He also said the records were built like songs. Verse, bridge, chorus, learned from 1980s pop and from ABBA, whose tracks he counted at four or five distinct sections. “Saved My Life,” as he described it to Warren while it played, still moves: it starts as one thing and becomes another. The complication was not only the number of slices. It was the number of places the track was willing to go, with the slices doing the work a chord part would have done if he had written one. He was frank, in the same lecture, about a weakness in the habit. Early on, if a section did not feel strong, he added a second, a third, a fourth, out of insecurity that the listener would not be satisfied. The excess and the ear are in the same account. He wanted the collage to make harmonic sense. He also sometimes added parts because he was afraid a part was not enough.

The older sound, asked for by name

By the time London was treating the cut-up records as a foundation, he had moved. Masters at Work had gone from a run of remixes into live bass players and disco. Basement Jaxx, he said, pushed him the same way. He started using samples to imitate a disco band rather than to chop a voice into a percussion instrument. A remix on Nervous, “Panther Party,” was, in his telling, a number one in the dance magazines, DJ Magazine among them. A remix of Wildchild’s “Jump to My Beat” was the first of that run to land, and the remixes that followed were the disco ones. Then his manager told him something was happening in England. People were picking up the older sound. His first response, as he reported it, was not pleasure. He had moved on, and now he was being asked to go back and relearn a style. Once he did, he said, he was glad. The return is where the tracks got more complicated. He started sampling from the 1960s instead of the 1970s. He said he learned more about music by sampling it than by sitting and listening to it. Mozart came up as a comparison he liked: a lot of notes, all of them having to work together. A collage, in that account, is not a mess that happens to be rhythmic. It is a piece in which the number of events is the point, provided they agree.

Asked which of his records had weighed on what became 2-step, he separated himself from the wave. People associated him with 2-step. He had, he said, nothing to do with its onslaught. He began to name a producer who had remixed “Never Let You Go,” and he could not remember the name. The records he was willing to claim, for impact in Britain, were “Saved My Life,” the “Alabama Blues” remix, and a remix of Sounds of One’s “As I Am.” The people he actually knew were specific. Tuff Jam: Karl “Tuff Enuff” Brown and Matt “Jam” Lamont. He worked in Brown’s studio. They came to his studio in Jersey, the word he used, with the records already out on New York labels. The first time he met them in person was in 1998, at the Winter Music Conference. DJ EZ was, in Edwards’ wording, his biggest supporter. A 4x4 gig at Time & Envy was the first time the size of the audience was a fact he had to stand in. He thought the year was around 2002 or 2003, and he was not sure. He remembered about 1,500 people.

One record in the lecture shows the method meeting a copyright department. Talking about a choir sound, he said it came from the first track of Stevie Wonder’s Songs in the Key of Life, and that when that piece came out on London Records he had to re-sing most of the parts. A sample can be the composition until the release requires a voice that can be signed for. He re-sang. He also told Warren to treat the released version as re-sung, not as the samples. The artifact and the clearance are two different endings for the same idea. In the EPS, a voice is cut until it fits the seconds. On the London release, a voice is performed again so the record can be sold. Both are decisions about custody. Neither of them is the shuffle.

House, sped up by someone else

The lecture page credits him with co-producing and singing on Daft Punk’s “Face to Face,” from Discovery, in 2001. In the room he talked about a later Daft Punk session, on Random Access Memories, and said the cut-ups were still audible and no longer sloppy. They had been polished until the method could sit inside a different kind of record. That is a later fact about an earlier technique. It does not change the 1995 12-inch. IR-117 is one side, on a New York house label, from a producer who told a lecture twenty years later that he had been trying to make house. The speeding-up was England’s. The name “speed garage” was, in his words, not exactly a positive phrase. The 2-step records that followed were, by his account, not his doing. What he did own was a sampler with a hard triplet and a memory measured in seconds, a habit of pitching 33s up to 45s when the memory filled, and a decision, taken from MK and from Enya and then pushed further, that a voice could be the instrument rather than the person in front of the instrument.

A syllable that has been sliced, pitched, and put back on a shuffle is not a singer finishing a sentence. It is a drum that used to be a word. The interest of “Saved My Life” is that he has described both the night it came from and the machine it had to fit, and that he has also described the moment a second country asked him to do the early thing again after he had tried to leave it. The manager who forced the record out in the first place is the same role, later, telling him that England wanted the old records. He went back. He told Warren the revisiting made the collages more serious, not less. The seconds on the EPS did not grow just because a scene had started to use the results. When the vocal would not fit, he borrowed someone else’s keyboard. When the phrase would not fit, he sped the record up and accepted the artifact on the way back down. The genre name came afterwards, in another city, at a tempo he did not choose. The 12-inch with his name on it was already out, one-sided, with a shuffle he has said came from the quantise.
