The Floor Already Knew

Honey Dijon's Black Girl Magic, on Classic Music Company: a house album that casts guests without becoming a compilation.

posted by cj archer · Nov 18, 2022

Back Catalogue · 18 Nov 2022

Honey Dijon’s Black Girl Magic came out on Classic Music Company, the label run out of the circle around Luke Solomon and Derrick Carter’s long house project. It is her second album. The guest list on the record, as this magazine first noted and as the sleeve confirms, includes Eve on “In the Club,” Pabllo Vittar and Urias on “Everybody,” Channel Tres, Mike Dunn, Luke Solomon. “Work” is the track that states the thesis in the plainest title on the album. A guest list of that range could have produced a compilation with a famous DJ’s name on the cover. It produced an album, which is a different editorial achievement.

Dijon is a Chicago-born DJ and producer who became one of the public faces of New York house, a resident and a remixer and a person whose sets were the argument long before the album was. House music’s origin is Black, queer, and specific: Chicago, then New York, warehouses and clubs, a music built by people the wider industry treated as a trend when it bothered to notice. Any review that skips that in order to talk about kick drums is doing publicity for amnesia. Black Girl Magic does not skip it. The title is the context. The music is house music in the sense that still means a song, a bassline, and a room, rather than a build and a drop with house used as a flavor.

Classic Music Company is a useful home for that meaning because the label’s identity is house as a continuing form, not house as a 1990s reissue campaign. Putting Eve, a rapper whose voice carries a different radio history, on a house track is a cast decision. Putting Pabllo Vittar, a Brazilian artist whose stardom sits far from the average “underground house” bill, on the same album is another. The risk with guests is that each track becomes that guest’s genre for four minutes and the album loses its hand. The hand here is the production and the sequence: a kick that stays literate, a sense that the same night could contain a rap verse and a melodic vocal without a press release explaining the range. Mike Dunn’s presence ties the record back to Chicago in a lineage way, not a sample way. Luke Solomon’s presence ties it to the label. Neither guest is there to authenticate her. She is the authentication. The guests are the party.

“She did not come to rescue house” was the right instinct in the short note this page used to be, and it deserves a fuller sentence. Rescue implies the music was waiting for a famous person to save it. House was not waiting. Dijon had been playing it, for years, in rooms where the music was the point and the photograph was incidental. An album, in that career, is not a debut. It is a designed night that can be repeated without the DJ standing there. That is a hard thing to make. A lot of DJ albums fail it by becoming souvenirs of a taste. This one tries to be the night itself: “Work,” “In the Club,” a title like “Everybody” that is either a threat or an invitation depending on the door policy. Club, in her usage, is not a concept. It is the place the songs assume.

The critical measure is whether the album holds when you do not know the guests’ names. On the strongest tracks, yes. The kick is doing what a house kick is supposed to do, which is to make time feel physical and shared. The songs have subjects — work, presence, a room full of people — that do not require a glossary. Where it leans on charisma, it leans on hers as much as on the features. That balance is the difference between a host and a curator. A curator arranges other people’s auras. A host has a room and lets people into it. Black Girl Magic is a host record.

In a zine that spends many of its pages on British continuations of Black American dance music — garage, jungle, house records made in Cumberland and Athens — this album is a necessary center, not a detour. The center is not a moral. The center is the form. If you want to know what the word “house” is doing on our own release pages, listen to a record that uses it without anxiety and with a guest list that reflects the music’s actual range. Then listen to the kick at the four-minute mark, when a lesser track would have already started begging for a breakdown. She lets it work.

cj archer
