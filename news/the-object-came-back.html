You Have to Flip It

Luminate's first-half-of-2026 figures said physical revenue was up 26 percent. The object came back. The reason to buy it did not change.

In the first half of 2026, Luminate’s figures — the ones that landed in public discussion by the time this page ran in September — put physical music revenue up 26 percent. Vinyl was climbing. CDs were back in the conversation, including the awkward fact, also from that reporting, that a large share of millennial CD buyers did not own a player. The number is the kind of number the industry loves, because it sounds like a plot twist. The plot twist is smaller when you remember why people buy objects. They buy them so the night has a spine. A file can be the record, as this magazine has argued. An object is a decision you can hold while you are not listening, which is most of the time.

The player-less CD buyer is not a paradox if you stop defining ownership as playback. Playback is one job. The other jobs are older: a gift, a shelf that shows a guest who you are when you are not explaining yourself, a tour edition you bought because you were in the room, a disc that survives a service shutting down. Some of those jobs are vanity. Vanity is allowed to fund a pressing plant. The plant does not know whether you own a tray to put the disc in. It knows you paid. The snobbery that insists a format only counts if you use it in the canonical way is the same snobbery that, in 2021, could not see a WAV as a real copy. Use is various. Payment is the consistent part.

What did change, and what the percentage blurs, is where the object is bought. Independent shops spent the 2010s rebuilding a reason to exist after the first digital collapse, then spent the early 2020s watching vinyl become a big-box product with exclusive colorways. A Target variant is not the enemy of music. It is a different door, with a different cut of the money and a different chance that the buyer will ever see a record store. The comeback is real and the raid is real. Both can be true in the same half-year. A shop that had a dead section in 2021 and a waitlist for dance 12-inches later is a local fact this page has reported in general terms; it is not a statistic to stretch. The visible pattern is enough: the object returned, and the corporations that do not otherwise run scenes were happy to sell it.

Cassette plants laughing, then not laughing, at a run of a couple of hundred is the small-label version of the same story. A tape is a bad format in several technical ways and a good one in a single social way. It is cheap enough to edition, physical enough to sell out, awkward enough that owning it means you meant to. Club Copy’s first cassette, You Are (Love), sold out. That fact is a data point of one. It matches the wider return of the object without proving anything about Luminate’s 26 percent. One tape is a decision a few people made. The percentage is a decision a market made. Writers should not use one to decorate the other.

Streaming pays, poorly, for the play. The object pays, once, for the keeping. A sane listener uses both and refuses the sermon that says one of them is a personality. The sermon is having a moment because the numbers moved. Numbers move for reasons that include Taylor Swift variants and Record Store Day and the simple fact that a generation that grew up on files got curious about weight. Curiosity is a good reason to buy a record and a bad reason to announce the end of the file. You will still send your friends links. You will still, if you are this label’s customer, download a WAV after checkout. The sleeve on the kitchen table is an extra copy with a job the WAV does not want. Let it do that job. Do not make it a manifesto.

The first-half figures will be replaced by another year’s figures, and someone will say the object left again or came back harder. The durable part is the spine. A night you paid for twice — once as a ticket or a drink, once as a disc you can find without logging in — is a night with a backbone. The disc can be a CD you cannot play. The backbone, then, is the memory and the money, which is less romantic and more accurate. Buy the format you will actually live with. Pay the people who made it, at the door that gets them the money. The percentage can stay in Luminate’s report, where it is doing its job.
