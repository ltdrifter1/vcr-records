Essential Listening: The Last Emperor

CJ Archer

Before the Oscars talk, put on Ryuichi Sakamoto’s music from The Last Emperor (1987). Bertolucci’s film needed a sound that could hold imperial China, exile, and modern politics without turning into tourist pastiche. Sakamoto — with David Byrne and Cong Su — wrote one that actually listens.

Why it mattered culturally isn’t just the trophy case, though that part is real: the score won Best Original Score, and Sakamoto became the first Asian composer to take that Oscar. Coming from Yellow Magic Orchestra into Bertolucci’s epic, he proved a Japanese electronic musician could write for Hollywood prestige without sanding off his ear. The win cracked a door that had been mostly shut.

The deeper shift was in the room itself. Traditional Chinese instruments sit inside Western leitmotif writing like they belong there — not as garnish, as story. After The Last Emperor, East–West film scoring stopped sounding like a special effect and started sounding like an option. A lot of later “crossover” soundtracks are walking a path this record opened without always saying so.

Listen away from the movie once. The themes still move like memory — clear, a little distant, hard to rush. Sakamoto’s gift was making big history feel private. That privacy is why the record still lands with producers and film heads alike: it’s cinematic without getting loud about it.
