The Fat of the Land

The Prodigy’s third album, released 30 June 1997 on XL, took breakbeat rave to number one in Britain and on the Billboard 200.

The singles had already won

The Fat of the Land is the third studio album by the Prodigy, released by XL on 30 June 1997. The catalogue number is XL121. Liam Howlett produced it. He recorded between 1995 and 1997 at Strongroom in London and at Earthbound in Braintree, Essex. It runs 56 minutes and 21 seconds. It went to number one on the UK albums chart and to number one on the Billboard 200. By 2019 it had sold more than ten million copies worldwide. Those figures are the scale. The music is the reason the scale happened: big-room breakbeat, distorted bass, and voices that sound like a confrontation.

The singles were hits before the album gathered them. Firestarter came out on 18 March 1996, Keith Flint up front, a guitar stab and a break, a video that made his look part of the song’s aggression. Breathe came out on 11 November 1996, Flint and Maxim, a darker bounce, a chorus that is a command. Smack My Bitch Up followed the album on 17 November 1997. The title is a catalogue fact and a provocation the group treated as a sample’s attitude rather than as a slogan they were willing to defend in any grown-up way. The track itself is a breakbeat instrument with a shouted line. The two earlier singles are the album’s true engines. By the time XL released the full length, the audience had already decided. The album’s job was to be as large as the singles and to have a few more rooms.

A hall, not a pirate station

The sound is rave music rearranged for the center of the chart without losing the distortion. Howlett’s programming is dense. Breaks are chopped until they feel like a machine arguing. Bass is rude and melodic. Flint’s voice is a snarl, not a singer’s instrument in the soul sense, and it is exactly right for Firestarter, where the point is adrenaline and a hook you can shout. Maxim’s voice is the other pole, toasted, rhythmic, a dancehall attitude inside a British break. Instrumental stretches, Climbatize and Narayan among them, prove the album can sustain a mood when nobody is yelling. The overall feeling is a hall. Pirate-radio jungle is a cousin, narrower and faster. This is the wide version, built to work in a field and on a television.

What it changed is the ceiling. British dance had reached number one before. It had not, in this form, occupied the top of the British and American album charts in the same season with a record this abrasive. Experience had been the rave album. Music for the Jilted Generation had been the long, stranger second act. The Fat of the Land is the popular one, and it is popular without becoming mild. Ten million copies is a pop number attached to a breakbeat record. The tension is the legacy. You can hear the album as a victory for the underground or as the moment the underground’s loudest group became a stadium act. Both are audible. The beats did not get polite.

Braintree is not a myth in the credits. Earthbound is a studio in Essex, and Strongroom is a studio in London, and the album sounds like hours in both: programmed, loud, revised until the break hits the same way on a club system and on a radio. The geography is ordinary. The result was not.

Five years from now Firestarter will still start fires in rooms that only know the video, and the album will still be the better context: Essex and London, 1995 to 1997, Howlett at the sequencer, Flint and Maxim as the voices, XL121, number one twice over. Play Breathe if you want the tension. Play the instrumentals if you want the producer without the shout. The fat of the land, in the proverb, is the best of what a place can grow. Howlett’s joke is that the best of it was a distorted break. The joke has held.
