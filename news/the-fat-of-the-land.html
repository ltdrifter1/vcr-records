The Fat of the Land

The Prodigy’s third album was released on 30 June 1997 through XL, catalogue XL121. It topped the UK albums chart and the US Billboard 200. Liam Howlett produced it.

The Fat of the Land came out on 30 June 1997 on XL Recordings. The band’s shop page at XL gives the catalogue number XL121 and the same date. Wikipedia calls it the group’s third studio album, produced by Liam Howlett, recorded 1995–1997 at Strongroom in London and Earthbound in Braintree, Essex — the same two rooms as Music for the Jilted Generation, which had come out on 4 July 1994. The length is 56 minutes and 21 seconds. It topped the UK albums chart and the Billboard 200. The page says that as of 2019 it had sold over 10 million copies worldwide, and that it is the group’s best seller. Three years and a few weeks separate the second album and the third. The rooms did not change. The commercial result did.

The singles are not June. “Firestarter” is 18 March 1996. “Breathe” is 11 November 1996. “Smack My Bitch Up” is 17 November 1997. The album arrives after two singles that were already hits in their own years, and before a third single whose title this page will print once, as a catalogue fact, and not dwell on. A single title can be a provocation and still be the name on the release list. The album’s month contains neither the March single nor the November one. It contains the album that collected a campaign already underway. Jilted Generation’s “No Good (Start the Dance),” filed in May of this magazine, had done the same job for the previous album: a single first, the long form after. The Prodigy’s method, across both records, was to let songs live as singles before the album date. June 30 is not the beginning of the campaign. It is the date the campaign became an album with a catalogue number.

Number one in Britain and number one on the Billboard 200 is a different scale from New Forms’ UK number 8 in the same month. Scale is not a judgment of which record a specialist prefers. It is a description of how far each object travelled in the general market. XL121 travelled further, on those two charts, than a drum-and-bass double album on Talkin’ Loud. Both were British, both were dance records in the broad sense, both were released in the last eight days of June 1997. The coincidence is the month’s. The methods are not the same. Howlett is a single producer credit on this album. New Forms prints four. One album is under an hour. The other is over two. Comparing them is fair if the comparison stays with those differences and does not end in a winner.

Earthbound in Braintree is the detail that keeps the number-one album attached to a town. A record that tops an American chart can still be made in a studio in Essex and a studio in London. The global sales figure does not move the rooms. Strongroom, which also appears on Underworld’s January 1994 album in this run, is a workplace more than one famous record passed through. Infrastructure is the unglamorous reading, and the accurate one. XL is the label that issued both Jilted Generation and this album. The continuity is a label and two studios and a producer, across three years, ending in a chart position the second album had not asked of the same rooms.
