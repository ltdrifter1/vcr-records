The room that named the music

Frankie Knuckles was born Francis Warren Nicholls Jr. in New York on 18 January 1955. He died in Chicago on 31 March 2014, aged 59. The obituaries called him the godfather of house. The phrase was theirs, and the city’s, and it stuck because the work had already stuck. In the 1970s and early 1980s he was the resident DJ at the Warehouse, a club in Chicago whose name the music took. House was the shortened form of that room. He played disco when disco was being declared dead elsewhere, extended records, mixed them into a night, and helped invent the idea that a DJ could be the author of an evening. When the evening needed records that did not exist yet, he began making them.

Your Love is the record to carry. Jamie Principle wrote and sang it. Knuckles’s version, issued on Trax, is the one that became a foundation: a walking bassline, a drum machine keeping civil time, a vocal that pleads without camping the plea. The lyric is simple. The arrangement is the Chicago house template in a form you can still teach with. Kick, hat, clap, a bass that moves like a person walking home, a voice in love and slightly desperate. Later producers added pads, stabs, and irony. The template under those additions is this. Baby Wants to Ride, another collaboration with Principle, is the companion, stranger and more explicit, the same machines put to a different lyric. Together they show a producer who understood that house was a song form, not only a DJ tool.

What the night required

The sound of those records is dry and human. The machines are not trying to imitate an orchestra. They are trying to keep a floor moving at a tempo where a singer can still be heard. Knuckles’s mixes and productions leave space. A piano chord can stab and then get out of the way. A string line, when it appears, supports the voice instead of replacing it. This is the opposite of a maximal rave record, and it is the reason the music travelled. New York, where he had come from, and London, where the records were imported and then imitated, could hear a feeling in the economy. You did not need the Warehouse’s membership rules to understand Your Love. You needed a system and a room.

What he changed is the job of the dance producer. A DJ who had been the night’s editor became a maker of the records the night required, and he did it with singers, especially Principle, rather than with anonymity. The godfather title, repeated in every obituary on 31 March 2014 and in the days after, is a burden, and it is accurate. Burden, because it can freeze a person into a statue. Accuracy, because the children of the form are everywhere: every house bassline that walks, every vocal that pleads over a machine, every club that believes a DJ is an artist. Chicago named a street for him. The better monument is the 12-inch.

The death closed a biography. It did not close a dancefloor. Clubs in Chicago and far from Chicago kept playing the same bassline, because a bassline is not an obituary. The godfather title can sit on a plaque. Your Love sits on a mixer, which is the more demanding memorial, the one that has to work at 1 a.m. on a Tuesday as well as on the day the news broke.

Five years from now Your Love will still be the cleanest explanation of why the music is called what it is called. A room in Chicago, a DJ from New York, a singer’s song, a bassline, a death in March that closed the biography and did nothing to the groove. Play the Trax version loud enough that the bass walks. The plea is still a plea. The machines are still patient. House music has been faster, ruder, more expensive, and more theoretical since. When it is good, it still remembers this pace.
