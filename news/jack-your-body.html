Jack Your Body

Steve “Silk” Hurley’s single spent two weeks at number one in January 1987, and British pop had to make room for a Chicago house record with almost no lyrics.

A chant and a pattern

Jack Your Body is a house single by Steve “Silk” Hurley, a Chicago producer working in the first wave of the music that came out of that city’s clubs and onto its own labels. In January 1987 it reached number one on the UK singles chart and stayed there for two weeks. It was the first house record to do that. Farley “Jackmaster” Funk’s Love Can’t Turn Around, with Darryl Pandy’s gospel shout, had already cracked the British top ten in 1986. Hurley’s record finished the job. A music that had been a local dialect of disco, made on drum machines and basslines in apartments and in the Warehouse’s wake, was now the most popular single in Britain.

The record itself is almost nothing, which is the point. There is a drum-machine pattern, the jack of Chicago house: a kick, a snare that snaps on the off-beat, a hi-hat rushing, a bass that pumps rather than sings. The lyric is the title, repeated until the instruction becomes the song. Jack is a dance, a physical verb, and a scene. Hurley does not build a verse, a bridge, and a moral. He builds a loop that a room can live inside, and he lets the loop be the composition. Heard now, it can seem bald. That baldness is the craft. Every later house record that strips a track down to a command and a rhythm is standing on this one.

How it crossed

Alexis Petridis, writing in the Guardian on 15 June 2011, put the week in its plain clothes. The record arrived with no conventional pop campaign. The video was cut from old black-and-white film. There were barely any lyrics to interview a singer about, because there was barely a singer. The chart still moved. Official Charts catalogues the single as LON117. It entered in January 1987, held the top for those two weeks, and was still on the chart into the first week of March. The object that did this was a specialist dance 12-inch that pop radio was not built to love. It won anyway, because the pattern was undeniable once a room had heard it, and because Britain in that winter was ready to dance to an import.

What it changed is easy to understate, because house later became ordinary. In January 1987 it was not ordinary. A Chicago producer with a drum machine and a one-line chant had outrun the bands, the ballads, and the rest of the week’s pop. The door that Love Can’t Turn Around had pushed was now open. British rave, the acid-house summers, the pirate stations, the warehouse parties: none of them is caused by a single week at number one. All of them become more imaginable once a house record has been the national number one and the country has not come apart. The chart is a blunt instrument. Sometimes a blunt instrument is the right evidence.

The sound is worth keeping in the ear for a reason that has nothing to do with the anniversary. Jack Your Body is a lesson in economy. The bass does one job. The drums do one job. The voice does one job, and the job is the title. There is no arrangement to hide behind if the groove is weak, and the groove is not weak. Five years from now the single will still be short, still nearly wordless, and still more useful than records with ten times the parts. Play it next to a modern house track that has remembered every plugin and forgotten the jack. Hurley’s record will sound like the instruction it always was. The body is supposed to answer. The rest of the century of dance music is, in one line, that answer getting longer.
