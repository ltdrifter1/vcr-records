Retired on 22 October 2026. The previous date, 27 October 2022, fell before 12 February 2024, the founding date on the Club Copy about page. The questions and answers below are not backed by a recording, a transcript, or any outside publication. They remain so the address still resolves. They are not a source.

This is the only interview Night Shift has published, and the subject is the editor. On 27 October 2022 CJ Archer answered five questions about Vancouver, the size of the label, the roster, the word “underground,” and what a new listener should play. The about page now says Club Copy was founded by Charlie Archer (CJ Archer). The j'adore sleeve credits him as producer. So the interview is not a visit from outside. It is the house talking to itself, early, before most of the records that now define the catalogue existed. The answers below are the ones printed then. They have not been improved. After them is the part 2022 could not know: what the pages say in 2026, and where the answers and the pages diverge.

There’s a certain kind of music that takes a while to find its way out. CJ Archer has spent years around it: records, half-finished projects, old machines, the Pacific Northwest underground that never needed a coastal magazine to exist. The conversation was about Club Copy, the weather, and why a finished record is usually a feeling somebody refused to throw away.

You started this from Vancouver. What does that actually sound like, besides rain?

It sounds like rooms that aren’t trying to be Brooklyn. Wet streets. A shop you can stand in too long. Ferries. The kind of night where the show is thirty people and somebody brought a cake. I like music that can live in that. If it only works in a caption, I’m not interested.

Club Copy is tiny on purpose. Is that a romance or a strategy?

Both, if I’m honest. Small editions mean you still know who might hold the thing. Catalogue numbers. A cassette that has to be dubbed. I’m not allergic to a bigger room. I just don’t want the music to start performing for one. The record club is the invite: first listens, a discount if you’re in, nothing shipped unless you choose it. That’s enough infrastructure for me.

Molly Haze, Forsberg, L.T. Drifta, Riscape: how do you know when someone’s ours?

I know when I want to leave it on. Not when I can explain it. Molly’s from Cumberland, house that feels like a private temperature. Forsberg’s Vancouver locked-drums, late-car energy. Drifta digs like it’s a practice. Riscape came in from Athens with piano in his hands and peak-time in his chest. None of them asked to match. They just all sound like they were finished by a person, not a moodboard.

What’s the part people get wrong about “underground”?

That it’s a costume. Or a purity test. Underground is just the work you do when nobody’s coming. Then if people come, you try not to sand off the thing that made you stay. Gossip, rooms, lyrics on trial, a club selling tickets and still saying no at the door: that’s all the same story. Who gets to keep the feeling, and who tries to checkout-button it.

Last one. What should somebody play tonight, if they’ve never been here?

j'adore if they want to be in a car. The Drifta mixtape if they want the house to keep going after they think they’re done. And if they want the long memory: Donuts, then whatever we put out that still has dust on it.

Read against the catalogue, the 2022 answers are partly prophecy and partly a map that went stale in specific places. The ferry sentence holds. Cumberland and Vancouver are separated by water, and the label’s Bandcamp now says Cumberland while several artist pages say Vancouver. Molly Haze’s page does put her in Cumberland, and the roster calls the music “UK garage made for sweaty dancefloors and staying out all night,” with a one-word bio, “k-i-s-s-i-n-g.” No Molly Haze release is listed. The “private temperature” line is the interview’s, not a discography. Forsberg “from Vancouver” is the sentence the label still prints and the sentence his Bandcamp contradicts: “House music from Cumberland, BC.” Locked drums and long loops survived; they are the artist-page copy in 2026. The city did not survive as a single fact. L.T. Drifta “digs like it’s a practice” is the answer that aged into something checkable. Nine titles, plus forty mixes on the mixes page, is a practice. Riscape from Athens with piano is exactly what his page says, conservatory included, and the peak-time claim is the roster’s. He has two Club Copy EPs, not a myth.

The last answer names j'adore and a Drifta mixtape and Donuts. j'adore would not be released until 10 August 2026, and when it arrived the producer credit was CJ Archer’s. Recommending it in 2022 means he was recommending work that was not yet a public Club Copy record, and recommending a record he would later be credited with producing. That is allowed in a conversation. It is a poor “start here” now, unless the reader wants the credit as part of the story. The Drifta mixes do exist, in public, on the mixes page. Donuts is the one recommendation that was already a finished, famous record in 2022 and is still the same record. J Dilla’s album does not depend on our catalogue. It is the stable object in an answer otherwise made of futures.

Tiny on purpose is the answer the shop can test. Catalogue numbers are real: the releases carry CC codes. A cassette is real at least once — You Are (Love), sold out, described on its page as the first Club Copy cassette. “Nothing shipped unless you choose it” matches the about page’s account of the record club. The strategy half of the answer is visible. The romance half is the interview itself: five questions, no outside voice, a subject who is the publisher. Underground, in his definition here, is work done when nobody is coming. A self-interview is a complicated example. Nobody from outside came. The work that holds up is the part a stranger can still put on: the numbered records, the mixes, the cassette that already sold through. The feeling he refused to throw away is not on the page. The records are.
