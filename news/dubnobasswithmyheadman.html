Dubnobasswithmyheadman

Underworld’s first album with Darren Emerson, released 24 January 1994 on Junior Boy’s Own, is a 73-minute city: techno, speech, and songs that refuse to end on time.

A group, rebuilt

Dubnobasswithmyheadman is the album that made Underworld a dance group. Karl Hyde and Rick Smith had already been a band, a synth-pop project with a different membership and a different aim. Darren Emerson joined, and the three of them recorded this record between 1991 and 1993 at Lemonworld and at Strongroom in London. Junior Boy’s Own released it on 24 January 1994. It runs 72 minutes and 53 seconds. The catalogue numbers are JBOLP 1, JBOCD 1, and JBOCS 1: the label’s first full-length, on vinyl, CD, and cassette. The title is the method. Dub, no, bass, with my head, man. A sentence collapsed into a word, the way the music collapses speech into rhythm.

The singles sketch the life of the record. Mmm… Skyscraper I Love You came out on 11 June 1993, months before the album, a long piece of city speech over a groove that seems able to circle a block forever. Dark & Long followed on 18 June 1994. Cowgirl arrived in September 1994. Dirty Epic in October 1994. The album is not a dump of those 12-inches. It is the place where they agree to live together, with the tracks that were never asked to be singles holding the corners.

Speech, weather, duration

The sound is Underworld’s sound before the film that made them famous, and it is already complete. Hyde’s voice is close and half-spoken, a man noticing a street rather than performing a confession. Phrases repeat until they turn into hooks without ever becoming slogans. Emerson’s influence is the weight: a techno low end, a kick that could work on a dancefloor, percussion that keeps the songs from drifting into atmosphere and staying there. Smith’s harmony is the part that makes the duration bearable. Chords change slowly. A track can run eight or nine minutes because the harmony has somewhere to go, not because a loop was left running.

Dirty Epic is the ballad, and it is still a dance track. The vocal is melodic, almost bruised, and the rhythm underneath refuses to drop into a rock ballad’s halt. Dark & Long is the other pole, a stretch of night-driving music, the title an honest description of both the mix and the feeling. Cowgirl is brighter, a chant with dirt on it. Skyscraper is the early manifesto: love song as inventory of a building, the voice listing and looping while the drums keep civic time. Together they argue that a techno album can have a lyric intelligence without borrowing a band format. There are no guitar solos to mark the emotional peak. The peak is a filter opening, or a sentence coming back.

Heard as a whole, the album is a city at night rather than a compilation of peaks. The tracks hand off. A spoken fragment in one piece answers a melody in another. The title’s jammed-together words are a fair description of the mix: dub space, a refusal, bass, a mind still talking. There is humor in the pile-up, and there is a serious claim under the humor. A dance record can be literate without stopping the drums.

What the record changed is Underworld’s job, and then a slice of British album-making. A group known, if at all, for an earlier pop life became the authors of a 73-minute dance record on an independent dance label. The next records, and the single that trainspotters and filmgoers can hum, sit on the far side of this one. Born Slippy .NUXX is later. It is a different object, a single with a film attached. Dubnobasswithmyheadman is the album that made that single possible, because it taught the group how to be long. Heard in five years, it will still sound like weather in a city: rain on the vocal, bass in the stomach, a man talking to himself at a tempo you can walk. The title remains the best review. Dub. Bass. A head, still on.
