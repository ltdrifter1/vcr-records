Dubnobasswithmyheadman

Underworld’s first album as a dance group came out on Junior Boy’s Own on 24 January 1994. The synth-pop band with the same name was already behind them.

Dubnobasswithmyheadman is Underworld’s third studio album and the first one that counts, if what you mean by Underworld is the group that played electronic dance music. Wikipedia dates the UK release to 24 January 1994, on Junior Boy’s Own. The running time listed there is 72 minutes and 53 seconds. The studios are Lemonworld and The Strongroom, both in London, with recording stretched from 1991 to 1993. Darren Emerson is on the album. He was not on the 1980s records. The page says this outright: the album is the first after the band had moved on from synth-pop, and the first to feature him.

A release database that tracks catalogue numbers puts three UK products on that same day. The double LP is Junior Boy’s Own JBOLP 1. The CD is JBOCD 1. The cassette is JBOCS 1. A promo double LP carries the same LP number. The object was not a single format with the others implied. On the day, vinyl, CD, and tape were all issued. That is a 1994 fact about how a dance album met a shop. The later reissues, including a 2014 anniversary campaign, are a different event. The January date belongs to the first three catalogue numbers.

The singles are older and younger than the album, which is the ordinary shape of this music and still worth writing down. “Mmm…Skyscraper I Love You” is dated 11 June 1993, seven months before the LP. “Dark & Long” is 18 June 1994. “Cowgirl” is September 1994. “Dirty Epic” is October 1994. The album sits in the middle of its own singles, not at the start of them. Anyone telling the story as a debut is telling the story of a lineup, not of a first recording. Change the Weather, the 1989 album, is still in the chronology. It is the record the new group had to be distinguished from. The title of the 1994 album is a sentence with the spaces taken out. The discography is a sentence with the dates left in.

Seventy-three minutes is a decision about the room. A record that long, issued on a double LP by a label whose name still sounded like a club rather than a major, was not aiming at the three-minute radio edit as its main form. The edits existed. They were the singles, on their own dates. The album was the other object: London studios, a new member, a label willing to press two discs, and a January release that let the spring singles trail it. Underworld’s later fame does not improve the catalogue numbers. JBOLP 1 is enough of a fact. The rest of the reputation can stand on the duration.
