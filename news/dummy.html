Dummy

Portishead's debut, Go! Beat, 22 August 1994: a Bristol album that made the sample feel like a voice.

Dummy is Portishead’s first album, released on Go! Beat on 22 August 1994. Geoff Barrow, Beth Gibbons, and Adrian Utley. Bristol. The Mercury Prize followed in 1995. The singles “Sour Times,” “Glory Box,” and “Numb” carried the record into rooms that had no intention of keeping up with British dance music, which is why it still works as a first album to hand someone. It does not require a scene. It requires a willingness to let a song be slow and not apologize.

The materials are concrete. Barrow came out of the same Bristol climate as Massive Attack’s early records: hip-hop drums, film samples, a suspicion of anything too clean. Utley is a guitarist and a musician in the older sense, which kept the band from being only a cratedigger’s project. Gibbons sings as if the microphone were a confidence she might withdraw. “Sour Times” leans on a Lalo Schifrin sample and a spy-film mood that would have been costume in weaker hands. “Glory Box” leans on Isaac Hayes. The samples are famous enough that naming them is not showing off. It is the point. Portishead used recognizable cinematic soul and then sang against it in a voice that made the recognition feel uneasy. The glamour and the dread arrive together.

“Roads,” “Mysterons,” “Wandering Star,” “Biscuit,” “It Could Be Sweet.” The album is sequenced as a night that never quite decides to go out. Tempos sit low. The drums are hip-hop drums, which matters: this is not a rock band that discovered a loop pedal. It is a hip-hop production sense applied to songs with a singer who could have fronted a very different group. That hybrid was promptly given the marketing name trip-hop and then punished for the name. Trip-hop became a section in shops and a way of saying “moody with a breakbeat,” and a lot of records that followed took the mood and left the rigor. Dummy’s rigor is the editing and Gibbons’s melodic decisions. She does not decorate the track. She sings a line you could remember, and then the track refuses to become the radio version of that memory.

Bristol in the early 1990s had a specific musical weather: sound-system culture, hip-hop, reggae, a generation of producers who had heard American records through British rooms. Portishead are part of that and not the whole of it. Massive Attack’s Blue Lines is the other door. What Dummy did, differently, was to make the internal life of one voice the subject, with the breakbeat as the world outside the voice. The spy-movie kitsch — theremin moods, minor keys, the visual style the band controlled so carefully — is easy to parody and was parodied. Under the parody is a singer who sounds like she means every phrase and a producer who will not let the band loosen up to prove they are having fun. Fun is not the job. Precision is.

The Mercury Prize made the album an official object, which helped and hindered. It helped because it kept the record in print and in the conversation. It hindered because official sadness is a tedious genre, and Dummy gets filed there by people who have not noticed how weird the drum programming is, or how much of “Glory Box” is a demand rather than a sigh. Gibbons is not sighing. She is stating terms. The arrangement’s languor is not compliance. It is control. Heard that way, the album is closer to soul music than to the dinner-party version of trip-hop, and soul is the tradition the samples were pointing at all along.

A previous note in this zine put the record in a Cumberland afternoon and used it as a local humidity. The album does not need the town. It was made in Bristol by three people with a clear, severe taste, and it has outlived the genre name that tried to hold it. Play “Sour Times” for the spy chord. Stay through “Roads,” where the album stops performing atmosphere and simply sings. If half the soft electronic music of the decades since has been chasing this humidity, the honest criticism is that most of it chased the reverb and missed the song. Portishead had both. That is rarer than the mood.

cj archer
