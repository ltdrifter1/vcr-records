Play

Moby’s fifth album, released by Mute on 17 May 1999, set Alan Lomax’s field singers inside electronic songs and then, slowly, everywhere else.

A last record that was not

Play is Moby’s fifth studio album. Mute released it internationally on 17 May 1999. V2 released it in North America on 1 June. He recorded it between August 1997 and early 1999 at his home studio on Mott Street in Manhattan, after Animal Rights, the 1996 guitar record, had convinced him he might be finished. Elektra had dropped him. He shopped Play and was refused until V2 said yes. He has said he sat in a park in March 1999, the sequence done, and thought the album would end his career. It did the opposite. The music is downtempo, techno, ambient, breakbeat, and a kind of electropop, built in large part on gospel and blues voices recorded in the field.

The voices are the moral and musical center. A friend lent him Sounds of the South, the 1993 box of Alan Lomax’s recordings. Honey is built on Bessie Jones. Find My Baby on Boy Blue. Natural Blues on Vera Hall. Why Does My Heart Feel So Bad? and Run On use old gospel. In the liner he thanks the Lomaxes and the archivists. The tracks are not documentary. They are songs. He writes grooves, chords, and pacing around a phrase until the phrase becomes a modern record and the singer, dead or distant, is present. Bodyrock is the hip-hop exception, with voices from Spoonie Gee and the Treacherous Three, a track he called essentially a rap song. Porcelain and South Side are his own lead vocals, melancholy and melodic. The second half of the album, by his own account of the sequence, dissolves. It starts energetic. It ends in a haze. That shape is audible. The first half gives you the singles. The second half gives you the room after the party.

Six thousand, then the world

The commercial life is the other half of the story, and it is not a straight line. The album debuted at number 33 in Britain and then fell. The first show, in the basement of the Virgin Megastore at Union Square, drew about forty people. Radio and MTV were largely uninterested. Moby and his managers licensed the tracks to films, television, and advertisements, because that was how anyone was going to hear them. He has said most of the licenses were not particularly lucrative and that the point was the hearing. The hearing worked. The album re-entered the British top 100 in January 2000, climbed, and in the spring spent five weeks at number one. It was the fifth best-selling album in Britain that year, and the best-selling independent album. By October 2000 it was platinum in seventeen countries. In the United States it peaked at number 38 on the Billboard 200 and sold more than two million. Worldwide it passed twelve million and became the biggest-selling electronica album. Eight singles carried it: Honey on 24 August 1998, Run On on 26 April 1999, Bodyrock on 12 July 1999, Why Does My Heart Feel So Bad? on 11 October 1999, Natural Blues on 6 March 2000, Porcelain on 25 April 2000, South Side on 10 October 2000, Find My Baby on 16 October 2000. The first six made the British top 40. South Side, remixed with Gwen Stefani, reached number 14 on the Hot 100.

The criticism at the time already heard the achievement and the risk. Metacritic’s score is 84, from 20 reviews. Robert Christgau argued that the sampled singers would not carry half as far without Moby’s grooves, pacing, and tunes. John Bush at AllMusic heard the early rave melodicism balanced against 1990s breakbeat techno. A dissenting Pitchfork review heard the field recordings’ raw magnetism cleaned up into something functional and disposable. The dissent points at a real tension. The album is beautiful, and the beauty is a kind of smoothing. What keeps it from being only a soundtrack is the sequence and the debt, which he printed. The singers are named by the thanks even when the track titles are his.

What Play changed is the distance between a specialist electronic album and the center of public sound. A Manhattan home recording, built on mid-century Southern voices, became unavoidable, in shops and in films and then, finally, on the radio that had ignored it. Five years from now the licenses will be a business story. The music is the voices and the chords. Play Natural Blues and the plea is still a plea, the electronic arrangement a lantern rather than a cage. Play the second half when the singles feel too familiar. The haze was always the rest of the record. Mute, 17 May 1999, Mott Street, twelve million copies later. He thought it was the end. It was the widest door electronic music had yet walked through.
