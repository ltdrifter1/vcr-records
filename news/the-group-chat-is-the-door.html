Are We Still Going

Resident Advisor lists the night and Dice sells the ticket. The address still moves the way it moved in 1998: person to person.

posted by cj archer · Mar 21, 2025

Resident Advisor is a magazine and a listings service. Dice is a ticket company. Both are useful and neither is the start of the night. The night starts when a specific person tells a specific other person where to be, and accepts the risk of having told them. In 1998 that was three missed calls and a phone that died on the way. In the mid-2000s it was a mass text with a back door and a name to give. In the years since it has been a thread with a pin. The tool changed. The politics did not: who has the address, who has the money, who will leave if the door is hostile, who is trusted not to bring the wrong crowd.

Calling this a discovery about social media misses the history. Dance music has always been semi-private at the point of entry, including when it was most public. A pirate station could tell you the record and not the warehouse. A flyer could tell you a phone number and the phone number could tell you a street. The secrecy was sometimes safety, sometimes snobbery, sometimes both. Women organizing nights have done the practical version forever, because the practical version is how you get your friends home. A scene that only remembers the promoter’s name on the flyer has forgotten the person who actually decided the list.

The listings site became a brochure when the night turned into content. You can browse a city you are not in and feel briefly that you belong to its weekend. Belonging, in that mode, is a feeling sold by a calendar. It is not nothing — people do find their first night that way, and a good listing is a public service — but it is not the same as being told, by someone who has decided you will not ruin it, that the door is on the left. The ticket app became a receipt. Receipts are good. They are how you prove you paid. They do not know whether the room was any good, and they cannot introduce you to the person you came to stand next to.

There is a snobbery available on the other side, and this magazine has indulged it: if you are not in the thread, you arrive as a tourist. Sometimes that is true. Often it is a way for people who got the address early to feel like a class. A healthy night has a door for the stranger. The stranger’s path might be the listing, the receipt, the friend of a friend. The path is not the problem. The problem is a culture that pretends the path is the culture, and a counter-culture that pretends the path is contamination. Both stories leave out the music. The music is why any of the paths are worth the trouble.

What the thread is good at, and what a listing is bad at, is the last-hour correction. The club has moved. The guest list is under a different name. Someone needs a charger and someone else is already in the car. That correction is the actual social life of a scene, and it is invisible to anyone writing the history from flyers alone. Historians of this music should treat the missed call and the pin as documents, even when the documents are gone. The flyer is what was willing to be public. The message is what got people in. Archives that only keep the public part will overrate promoters and underrate friends.

Are we going: the question is older than the app that currently carries it. It is the whole ethics of a night in one line. You answer it with your time and your money and your body, not with a reaction. If the messages go quiet, the night has started without you, or it has been cancelled, and you will find out which by whether you can hear your own house. Both outcomes are a kind of evening. Only one of them needs an address. The address is a responsibility. Whoever has it is running more of the culture than the logo on the listing.

cj archer
