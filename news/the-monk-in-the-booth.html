The Monk in the Booth

Before you can drop the beat, someone has to teach you how to sit still.

Label noteDec 18, 2025

CJ Archer

Before you can drop the beat, someone has to teach you how to sit still.

Underground hip-hop runs on hustle. Grind, drop, promote, repeat, and don't stop until the catalog says otherwise. So it's worth noticing when an artist steps out of that loop entirely — not to burn out quieter, but to sit down. DC producer and MC Born I spent last spring on tour with the monks and nuns of Plum Village, the monastery founded by Thich Nhat Hanh, performing what he calls a "Hip Hop Soundbath" — bars laid over singing bowls instead of drums, built for a room that's meant to breathe, not move. His most recent album, AMIDA, treats a verse the way a breath practice treats an exhale: something you don't rush, because rushing it defeats the point.

Born I comes up through the same lineage most of the scene claims — Wu-Tang, Andre 3000, Alice Coltrane — but he's spent as much time as a certified meditation teacher as he has behind a mic, leading retreats that use self-compassion and rhyme the same way a lot of us use a drum machine. His teacher told him, at some point, to drop the idea of being a "good" practitioner altogether. Not because the practice doesn't matter, but because performing the identity of someone who has it figured out is its own kind of noise. His label is named after the same lesson: You Are Enough. Not once you've blown up. Now.

It's a strange, necessary correction for a scene that treats stillness as a failure state — dead air on the timeline, a gap in the release calendar, proof you've stalled. Rooms are getting quieter on purpose lately: breathwork sets before the headliner, sound baths opening for the rave, DJs building fifteen minutes of near-silence into a peak-time set just to see who stays present for it. None of it is soft. It's the opposite of soft. Sitting in a room with nothing to hide behind takes more nerve than dropping a bass at the right moment ever did.

Every record we've put out here takes longer than it should by industry math, and we've never had a good answer for why beyond "it wasn't ready." Maybe that's the same thing Born I is teaching a room full of strangers with a singing bowl. The track doesn't need saving. Neither do you. Both just need the time to actually arrive.
