Buried Treasure

Digging is a method, not a personality. The find is the record you almost put back, and the shop is not a moral stage.

format
Essay
date
Sep 3, 2024
author
CJ Archer
words
799 words · 3 min
catalogue
CC003

A record shop on a wet afternoon is an ordinary room that a certain kind of listener has turned into a chapel. Cardboard, other people’s coffee, a bin of records nobody has alphabetized lately, a price in pencil. The romance written about this room is so thick that the room itself can be hard to see. Digging — the act of looking through stock you did not search for — is a method of finding music. It is not a personality. It is not a moral achievement. It is closer to reading the shelves of a library that happens to sell the books. Some days you leave with something that rearranges your week. Most days you leave with wet shoes and nothing.

The method has a history that does not belong to any one shop. Hip-hop producers dug because the music was built from existing records and the good ones were finite. Reggae selectors dug. House DJs dug. The crate became a symbol because it was a tool, the way a carpenter’s box becomes a symbol only after the tools have been romanticized by people who do not use them. Discogs and its cousins took the tool and made it a market with grades and alerts. The market is useful. It is also a place where the hunt is replaced by a search field, and where “rare” becomes a number you can sort. Rare is a market word. It tells you about supply. It does not tell you whether the B-side is any good.

What the shop still offers, when it offers anything, is accident inside a boundary. You are in one city, in one stock, with the hours you actually have. The accident is limited by what the buyer for the shop cared about and by what the people who sold their collections cared about. That is already an edit. Finding a record inside someone else’s edit is a different experience from being recommended a record by a system that has watched you. Both experiences can produce love. Only one of them involves your wrists and a stranger’s handwriting on a sleeve. The magazine version of this fact usually ends in a sermon about algorithms. The sermon is duller than the fact. A recommendation system is a tool with a business model. A bin is a tool with dust. Use the one you can stand in front of today. The moral is not required.

Buried treasure, as a phrase, flatters the finder. It implies a map, a chest, a deserved reward. Most of what you find is buried only in the sense that it was filed in the wrong place or priced without confidence. The side you keep is often the one you almost put back, because the cover was ugly or the name was unknown or you were tired. That near-miss is the part of the method worth describing accurately. Taste, in a shop, is a sequence of rejections and then one acceptance you cannot fully justify yet. The justification arrives at home, on a speaker, when the track either opens or does not. If it does not, you have bought a lesson for the price on the sticker. Lessons at that price are still cheaper than the personality you can build by talking about lessons.

Club Copy’s about line — “Music we want to keep” — is a shop sentence before it is a slogan. Keeping is the moment after the find, when the record is yours and the story of finding it starts to decay. The decay is healthy. A record that only works as a story about how you found it is a record you do not actually play. The ones that survive are boring in the best way: they go back on the shelf and come off the shelf, and the afternoon you found them becomes irrelevant. This zine has sometimes written the opposite, lingering on the wet shoes and the walk home as if the walk were the chorus. The chorus is on the disc. The walk is how you got it home without bending the jacket.

So the practice, stripped of chapel language, is this. Go to a room that holds more music than you can search. Reject almost all of it. Accept one thing for a reason you can test later. Pay the shop, not only the platform that will still have the file if the shop closes. Play the thing until you know whether you were right. If you were wrong, the treasure was not buried. It was ordinary stock, and ordinary stock is what a culture is mostly made of. The rare side you actually needed will still be there some other afternoon, misfiled, waiting for someone who is not performing the role of a person who digs.
