The Infamous

Mobb Deep’s second album came out on 25 April 1995 on Loud. Havoc produced most of it. Q-Tip produced and mixed. “Shook Ones, Part II” had already been out since February.

The Infamous was released on 25 April 1995 by Loud Records, with RCA and BMG on the label line. It runs 66 minutes and 51 seconds. The studios are Battery, Platinum Island, Firehouse, and Unique, in New York, the same city and some of the same rooms Illmatic had used the year before. Havoc produced most of the album. Q-Tip contributed production and mixed. Guests include Nas, Raekwon, Ghostface Killah, and Q-Tip. Executive producers include the group, Matt Life, and Schott Free. The single “Shook Ones, Part II” is dated 7 February 1995 and is filed in this magazine’s February volume. The album is April. The split is deliberate.

Sixty-seven minutes is the long rap album of the mid-1990s, against Illmatic’s forty. Both are New York debuts in the sense that matters to a listener — Illmatic a first album, The Infamous a second that functioned as the arrival — and they chose opposite lengths. Mobb Deep had already released Juvenile Hell in 1993. The Infamous is not a debut. Calling it one is a common slip this page will not repeat. It is the album on which the producer inside the group, Havoc, took the beats, and an outside producer from A Tribe Called Quest came in to produce and to mix. That combination is the instrumental fact. The culture that traces boom-bap as a craft has to count records where the beatmaker is also the rapper, and records where a famous beatmaker visits. This album is both.

Loud is the label, not Columbia. Illmatic’s major and Mobb Deep’s label are not the same kind of company, and the guest list crosses the gap: Nas appears here a year after his own Columbia debut. Guest verses are how New York rap of this period published its map. The map is not a scene report from a club. It is a credit block. Raekwon and Ghostface, in April 1995, are already Wu-Tang names; Raekwon’s own album, Only Built 4 Cuban Linx..., is still August. Q-Tip is already a producer with a group. The April album sits in the middle of other people’s calendars. That is what a credit block looks like when a city is actually talking to itself: not a manifesto, a list of names who had records due in other months.

The February single means a listener could know “Shook Ones, Part II” before they could buy the album. The album’s job in April was to put that song in a sequence of sixty-seven minutes and to show the rest of the production. Havoc’s credit is the sentence. A magazine interested in what instrumental practice sounds like when it is not separated from the song can stop at that sentence and at the rooms in New York. It does not need to perform the lyrics. The lyrics have been performed. The dates, the label, and the producer are the part that still gets left off the poster.
