Selected Ambient Works Volume II

Aphex Twin’s double album, released by Warp on 7 March 1994, has almost no titles and almost no beats, and it still went to number one on the British dance chart.

Photographs instead of names

Selected Ambient Works Volume II is the second studio album by Aphex Twin, Richard D. James. Warp released it in Britain on 7 March 1994, as a double CD, a triple LP, and a cassette. Sire released it elsewhere on 12 April. The original CD runs 156 minutes and 42 seconds. The LP and the cassette run 166 minutes and 53 seconds, because a track that sat on the vinyl and the tape was left off the original CDs. Later expanded editions run 184 minutes and 53 seconds. Most of the pieces have no titles. They are identified by photographs, textures, and the pie-chart diagrams that stood in for names. The refusal is the design. A music this long, and this quiet, would have been betrayed by a tracklist that shouted.

James described the recording, in a line that has stayed attached to the album, as standing in a power station on acid. The image is his, and it fits the sound: huge interior space, a hum that might be machinery or might be harmony, tones that seem to have been left running. This is not the ambient techno of his earlier Selected Ambient Works 85–92, which still had beats a DJ could hold. Volume II is beatless, or so nearly beatless that a pulse, when it appears, feels like a visitor. Piano figures drift. Chords hang. A piece can occupy ten minutes without a single event you could call a drop. The event is the chord changing, or the room tone shifting, or a melody that seems half-remembered from a music box.

A chart for a music without a chorus

Britain’s dance-album chart put it at number one, and it stayed in that chart’s top five for six weeks. The main albums chart put it at number 11. By July 1994 it had sold more than 60,000 copies outside the United States. Those numbers are the surprise. A double album of untitled, nearly beatless pieces, from a producer the press liked to photograph as a grin, outsold the week’s ordinary dance compilations on the specialist chart and dented the main one. Ambient music had been a critical category and a late-night radio fact. In March 1994 it was also a retail fact. Warp’s bet, that James’s audience would sit still, was correct.

The missing track on the original CD became a collector’s fact and a listening fact. The vinyl has a piece the first CDs do not. The expanded edition puts the absence back. None of that changes the character of the music, which is anonymity as a positive quality. Without titles, you cannot skip to the famous one, because fame has nothing to attach to except a picture or a number. You put the record on. You are in it. Some pieces are tender, a lullaby with the lights wrong. Some are uneasy, a drone that refuses to resolve into comfort. The range is the argument against the idea that ambient means pleasant background. This album can furnish a room. It can also make the room feel watched.

What it changed is the permission for electronic albums to be long, quiet, and commercially real. Producers who later made beatless records on dance labels were working in a space this one cleared, even when they never imitated the pie charts. James went on to louder, ruder, more titled music. Volume II remains the wide one. Five years from now it will still be the record you play when you want electronic music with nowhere to hide a weak idea. There is no break to distract you, no lyric to agree with, no single to extract. There is harmony, duration, and a photographer’s idea of a name. Warp released it on 7 March 1994. The power station is still humming.
