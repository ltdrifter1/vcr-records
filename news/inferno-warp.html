Thirteen years, then a tape

Inferno is the fifth studio album by Boards of Canada, Michael Sandison and Marcus Eoin. Warp released it on 29 May 2026. It is catalogue WARP496. The vinyl liner prints WARPLP496. They recorded it at Hexagon Sun in the Pentland Hills, the same Scottish workshop as the records that made them, and Matt Colton mastered it at Metropolis in London. It runs 70 minutes. It holds eighteen tracks. It is the first studio album since Tomorrow’s Harvest in 2013, a gap of thirteen years. The music is electronic, ambient, and the kind of hauntological instrumental writing the duo made their name on, pushed darker. Guitars and live drums sit beside the synths. Sampled voices, many of them religious or occult, sit beside the haze. Around half the tracks are instrumental. The other half speak, in other people’s mouths.

The campaign began in April 2026, before the title was public. Unlabeled VHS cassettes went to people who had bought physical records from Warp. Posters appeared in cities. A website the duo had used around Tomorrow’s Harvest came back with the words “nobody home…” in English and in Morse. On 16 April they shared a clip called Tape 05, the first new music in thirteen years. That piece of music is on the album as Deep Time. They announced Inferno on 22 April, with a 42-second trailer and a hexagon in shifting color. On 7 May, Warp released the opening pair as a single, Introit / Prophecy at 1420 MHz, with a video by Robert Beatty. Introit lasts 36 seconds. Prophecy at 1420 MHz lasts 5 minutes and 4 seconds and is the door: live drums, guitar, the duo’s usual fog cut by something sharper. Listening sessions followed on 22 May in Barcelona, Berlin, Glasgow, London, Los Angeles, New York, and Tokyo. There were no interviews. Rolling Stone called the marketing a noisy anti-campaign. Reviewers were given streams that could be played a handful of times. The formats are the ordinary Warp set and then one extra: digital, black double LP, CD, cassette, and a deluxe transparent red double LP with a hexagonal 8-inch flexi, Vol. 4 – P. Primers – 177 Giraud’s Mirror.

Guitars, sermons, a heartbeat

The sound is the Boards of Canada grain with the comfort turned down. Where Music Has the Right to Children could feel like a school programme remembered fondly, Inferno feels like the same classroom after the building has been put to a grimmer use. Pitchfork’s Philip Sherburne heard an unusually gothic cast in the guitars. The Financial Times’ Ludovic Hunter-Tilney heard drums and electric guitars pushing the music toward a post-punk chill. Prophecy at 1420 MHz is the proof, a rock instrumentarium inside the electronic one. Hydrogen Helium Lithium Leviathan leans on heavy synths. Naraka, named for a Sanskrit word for hell, drones, and carries Hare Krishna chanting in the mix of its voices. Deep Time, the old Tape 05, has strings and timpani, a short processional. Somewhere Right Now in the Future is the dream-pop exception, a lighter room in a dark house. I Saw Through Platonia closes the eighteen on a recording of a human heartbeat, 2 minutes and 39 seconds, the body as the last instrument.

The voices are doing thematic work, not wallpaper. The first half of the album is thick with them. Age of Capricorn moves through talk of an Antichrist and into a Christian televangelist’s sermon. Father and Son lifts a conversation from the 1971 documentary The Jesus Trip, a man telling his father he loves the Lord more than any physical being. All Reason Departs samples language in the orbit of Aleister Crowley, a magical operation, a new aeon. The Word Becomes Flesh includes a woman describing an embryo. The Process runs a synthesised female voice through something like word salad. Critics compared the sermon-sampling to Brian Eno and David Byrne’s My Life in the Bush of Ghosts, and they compared the album’s darkness to Geogaddi and to Tomorrow’s Harvest rather than to the sunnier 1998 record. Mark Richardson, in the Wall Street Journal, called the music bleak and beautiful, the mood apprehensive. Sasha Geffen, in Resident Advisor, heard a more direct menace than the generalised unease of the older albums, and also, against the year’s turmoil, some of the duo’s most placid and enchanting music. Both descriptions can be true. The placid stretches are uneasy. The uneasy stretches are beautifully made.

Metacritic’s aggregate, from 21 reviews, is 85, the band marked universal acclaim. AnyDecentMusic’s average from 21 reviews is 7.9. Pitchfork’s score is 8.6. Uncut and Clash both landed on 9. Beats Per Minute landed on 90. The dissent is real and useful. Ben Beaumont-Thomas, in the Guardian, called the album a big disappointment and heard a duo stuck in the past. Hunter-Tilney thought the apocalyptic theme was relevant and also a drag on the musicality. A listener can hear what they heard. The palette is the duo’s palette. The difference is the pressure: live drums, gothic guitar, a lyric world of scripture and magic, a closing heartbeat. Sandison is credited with the production and, on the physical release, with the writing of most of the tracks. Eoin is all over the instrumental and design credits, and he shares writing on Prophecy, Into the Magic Land, Blood in the Labyrinth, All Reason Departs, Arena Americanada, and The Process. The workshop is still two people. The liner is honest about who did the largest share.

The album reached number 3 in Britain, number 1 on the British dance and independent charts, and number 2 in Scotland. Australia put it at number 3. The Billboard 200 put it at number 29, and the US dance chart at number 1. A day before release, the White House used a piece of Deep Time in a political video. Warp answered that neither the label nor the duo condoned the unauthorised use of the music for political messaging. The answer belongs in the biography of the week. It does not exhaust the record. What Inferno changed is the silence. Thirteen years is long enough for a group to become a memory. The album refuses the memory treatment. It is new work, in the old studio, with a harsher brief: faith, biology, catastrophe, and the old tape-hiss tenderness still available when they want it. Five years from now the VHS mailout will be a story and the music will be the reason the story mattered. Play Prophecy at 1420 MHz for the drums and the guitar. Play Deep Time for the strings. Play through to the heartbeat. Eighteen tracks, seventy minutes, Hexagon Sun, 29 May 2026. The inferno in the title is not a cartoon of flames. It is a temperature the duo finally allowed the songs to reach.
