Leftism

Leftfield’s debut, out on 30 January 1995 through Hard Hands and Columbia, turned a run of early singles into one long argument for patience.

The singles came first

Leftism is the debut album by Leftfield, Neil Barnes and Paul Daley. It was released in Britain on 30 January 1995, on their own Hard Hands label and on Columbia, and it reached the United States on 15 August that year. They recorded it at Rollover Studios in London between 1992 and 1995. It runs 69 minutes and 37 seconds. Those facts are the frame. The music inside the frame is progressive house with the weight of dub: long, rolling, unwilling to arrive at a chorus and then leave.

The singles refuse the usual order, in which an album is finished and then carved into promotions. Song of Life is dated 30 November 1992. Open Up, with John Lydon, is 1 November 1993. Original followed the album on 13 March 1995. Afro-Left is 24 July 1995. Release the Pressure, rebuilt for the album, came back as a single on 8 January 1996. Release the Pressure and Song of Life had already existed on Hard Hands. The album versions were made again. The earlier vinyl of Release the Pressure was slower. Leftism is a second construction that knows the first one happened.

How it moves

The running order makes the claim before any review does. Release the Pressure runs 7:39. Afro-Left runs 7:33. Melt is 5:21, Song of Life 6:55, Original 6:22. Black Flute, at 3:46, is the short chapter in a book of long ones. A record that keeps most of its pieces past six minutes is asking for a duration, the length of a room rather than the length of a radio spot. The bass sits low and keeps moving. The drums are house drums, but they are in no hurry to stamp a drop into the middle of a track and call that the idea.

Open Up is the track a stranger is most likely to know, and it earns that. Lydon’s voice is a sneer with a purpose, set on a rolling progressive-house chassis instead of on a punk riff. The shock, even now, is how well the sneer fits. He sounds hired for the temperature, not pasted on for the headline. Afro-Left is the other public door: percussion up front, a chant that feels travelled, a groove that could run another few minutes without repeating itself into a wallpaper. Melt and Black Flute are the proof that the album is an album. One stretches. One stops early. Neither exists to feed a chart.

Progressive house is the name that stuck. On the UK gatefold vinyl, catalogue HANDLP2D, the same record is filed under electronic music and then under a stack that includes downtempo and progressive house. The limited triple vinyl, HANDLP2T, adds dub to that stack. The names are useful because the music really does borrow from all three. It has house’s kick, dub’s space, and a downtempo patience in the way a chord is allowed to sit. It is dance music for people who intend to stay.

What it changed

Open Up peaked at number 13 on the UK singles chart. Afro-Left reached number 20 on the US Club Play chart in 1995. The album sold more than 220,000 copies. Chart weeks and sales count different things, and both are modest beside the album’s afterlife. What Leftism changed was the idea of a British house album. Before it, the form lived on 12-inches and on compilations that pretended a dozen producers were one night. Barnes and Daley put their own label’s name beside a major’s, kept the early singles in the sequence, and remade two of them so the sequence would hold.

The catalogue numbers say the politics plainly. HANDLP2D and HANDLP2T still begin with the independent’s letters. Columbia is the reach. A duo who had been releasing records on their own label for years accepted a major’s distribution without retiring the label that had paid for the first versions. That pairing became a model for a certain kind of British dance career: keep the workshop, borrow the truck.

Thirty years on, the long pieces are still long. Progressive house has become a historical bin as well as a description, a shelf for records that would not hurry a mix. Leftism can sit on that shelf and still be more than the label. The early singles are still earlier than the album. The remakes are still remakes. A listener in five years will hear the same structure a listener heard in 1995: a record that believes attention is something you build with bass and minutes, not something you catch with a hook and spend. The useful way to hear it is from the start, loud enough that the low end is a physical fact. Black Flute will still feel like a door in the middle of a corridor. Open Up will still sound like a man who was not supposed to be on a house record and was, in fact, exactly who the record needed.
