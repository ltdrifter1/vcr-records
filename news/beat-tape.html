Beat Tape

A 22 August 2023 note announced a CJ Archer beat tape. The current Club Copy catalogue does not list one.

posted by cj archer · Aug 22, 2023

On 22 August 2023 this desk published a short note called “Beat Tape” and aimed it at CJ Archer, the producer the artist page now describes as based in Vancouver and as the founder of Club Copy. The note said the tape was short instrumentals, that nothing on it was begging to be a single, and that it was the place to hear the label before the later records got “prettier.” It linked to the artist page and the library. It did not link to a release page, because there wasn’t a release page to link. There still isn’t. The catalogue, as the site publishes it, runs from L.T. Drifta, Forsberg, and Riscape. No CJ Archer title appears among the albums, EPs, and singles for sale.

That absence is the story, and it is a better story than the atmosphere the note was selling. Beat tapes are a specific object in hip-hop. They are how producers circulate beats that are not yet songs: a sequence of instrumentals, often unfinished on purpose, often given away or sold cheaply, so a rapper or a listener can hear the maker’s hand without a single’s campaign. Stones Throw built a public around that object. So did a generation of producers who treated the DAT, the CD-R, and later the private link as the real discography, with the official album as a selection from it. When a label’s own writer announces a beat tape and the label’s shop never lists it, one of three things has happened. The tape was a plan. The tape existed and was withdrawn. Or the tape was a metaphor for a folder of loops, and the metaphor got a headline.

The 2023 note leans on the third reading and then talks as if the object were real. “Before the catalogue had a spine, there was a folder.” A folder is not a tape. A tape, even a digital one, has a sequence and a title and usually a date you can cite. This magazine cannot cite a tracklist it was never given, and it will not describe drums it cannot point a reader toward. What it can cite is the rest of CJ Archer’s public credit on the site. The about page says Club Copy was founded by Charlie Archer (CJ Archer) on the data’s founding date of 12 February 2024 — which is after this note. The j'adore page, released 10 August 2026, lists him as producer of Forsberg’s two-track single. The artist page offers a portrait-sized biography of one line: producer, Vancouver. Between a pre-founding note about a tape and a 2026 production credit on someone else’s single, the verifiable discography of the editor-as-producer is that credit. One single. Named. On a page anyone can open.

There is a critical habit, common to small imprints and to zines that share a staff with them, of treating the unreleased folder as the authentic work and the released record as the compromise. The habit feels romantic and it fails a simple test. Authenticity that cannot be played for a stranger is a private experience. It can be true and still be useless to an archive. Night Shift’s job, if the vision on the wall means anything, is the archive: pieces that are still worth reading when the mood has expired. A beat tape that cannot be found is not an archive. It is a gesture. The honest gesture, in 2026, is to say the August 2023 piece overclaimed, and to point at the credit that does exist.

j'adore is not a beat tape. It is a finished house single, two songs, produced by the person the tape note was about, released under another artist’s name. That may be what “the later records got prettier” was trying, clumsily, to predict. Pretty is the wrong word for a production credit. Specific is the right one. Drums someone else performs inside, loops that end, a title, a price, a date. If a CJ Archer instrumental tape surfaces with a tracklist and a page, it can have a review of its own, and this essay can be a footnote to it. Until then, the footnote is the essay. The founder’s audible contribution to the catalogue, on the pages we have, is the production of CC007. Start there. The folder can stay a folder.
