June, twice

Surrender is the third studio album by the Chemical Brothers, Tom Rowlands and Ed Simons. It came out in Japan on 7 June 1999 and in Britain on 21 June, on Freestyle Dust and Virgin. It runs just under an hour. It is big beat, electronica, and psychedelia, and it moves further into house than the records before it. It was their second British number-one album. The BPI certified it double platinum on 30 September 2005. Pitchfork scored it 9.0. NME scored it 8. The guests are the other headline: Noel Gallagher on Let Forever Be, Hope Sandoval on Asleep from Day, Bernard Sumner on Out of Control, Jonathan Donahue on Dream On, Bobby Gillespie’s backing voice on Out of Control. Beth Orton, who had sung on the earlier albums, is absent. The absence is part of the change of air.

The singles frame a year. Under the Influence had already circulated in June 1998 as Electronic Battle Weapon 3, a vinyl tool for DJs, before it had a public name. Hey Boy Hey Girl, the first official single, came out on 26 May 1999 and reached number 3 in Britain, a rave record in the old sense: a sample, a shout, a kick, a grin. Let Forever Be followed on 23 July and reached number 9, Gallagher’s voice on a track that nods toward the Beatles without dressing up as them. Out of Control came out on 8 October and reached number 21, Sumner singing, the track the duo themselves described as the house-influenced one. Music: Response waited until 6 March 2000 and was kept off the British singles chart because it carried five songs, more than the three the chart then allowed. A bureaucratic fact, and a useful one. The group were still thinking in DJ tools as well as in singles.

A rave, a guest, a long middle

The sound is the Chemical Brothers’ scale applied to a wider set of temperatures. Hey Boy Hey Girl is the festival door, irresistible and simple, the block-rocking inheritance in a tighter suit. Let Forever Be is a song, three minutes and change, a guest used as a melody rather than as a celebrity stamp. Out of Control runs past seven minutes and earns it, a house progression with Sumner’s melancholic top line and Gillespie somewhere in the stack. The Sunshine Underground is the long psychedelic center, more than eight minutes. Rowlands has said the title came from psychedelic poster books and that they wanted it to sound like a live band finding its feet, influenced by a Spiritualized live version, a composite of takes with a hyperspace passage in the middle. Asleep from Day gives Sandoval the quiet room, her voice a Mazzy Star hush inside their machines. Dream On, with Donahue, ends the sequence and hides a reprise. The album is sequenced as a night that peaks, wanders, and then lies down.

Kate Gibb made the cover, a screen print from a Richard Young photograph of William Jellett, the dancing fan known as Jesus Among the Fans, taken at Kensington Olympia in 1976. Simons liked the picture because everyone else is sitting and one person is getting it. The image is the right one. Surrender is an album about that person, the listener who is already standing, and about the producers’ decision to give that person house, psychedelia, and actual singers rather than another hour of the same break.

What it changed is the duo’s range at the exact moment they could have repeated Dig Your Own Hole and been thanked for it. They took the number-one slot with a record that includes a rave single, a seven-minute house song, a Mazzy Star guest, and an eight-minute trip. Five years from now Hey Boy Hey Girl will still fill a room in thirty seconds. The album is the reason to stay after it. Japan on 7 June 1999, Britain on 21 June, Freestyle Dust, a second number one, Gibb’s dancer. The surrender in the title is not a defeat. It is the moment the track stops fighting you and you are in it.
