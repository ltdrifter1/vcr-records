Black Secret Technology

A Guy Called Gerald’s album, released on Juice Box in March 1995, argued that jungle could be a long soul record and not only a 12-inch.

March, and a full length

Black Secret Technology is an album by A Guy Called Gerald, Gerald Simpson, released on his Juice Box label in March 1995. No day of the month has settled into the public account, and the month is enough: it is a March record, British, and it arrived when jungle was still mostly a music of 12-inches, dubplates, and pirate radio. The album runs 77 minutes and 57 seconds. He recorded it at the Machine Room in England. On the dance-album chart dated 1 April 1995 it stood at number 8. His previous album, 28 Gun Bad Boy, had come out in 1992, a harder, more broken statement. Finley’s Rainbow, a single, had come in 1994. So Many Dreams followed in 1996. Between the early rave hit that first put his name in the paper and this album, he had become a producer of jungle with a singer’s instinct.

The sound is drum and bass that refuses to treat the vocal as a sample to be chopped and discarded. Breaks are fast, the Amen and its cousins rearranged until they roll rather than attack. Bass is sub and melody at once. Over that, voices sing. They plead, they phrase, they stay in the track long enough to be people. The result is soul music at a tempo soul radio would not have recognised, and jungle with a warmth that the stricter darkside records of the same months had set aside. Simpson’s programming is the constant. He had come up in 808 State’s first chapter and then left to make his own path. By 1995 the path was this: long pieces, female vocals up front, a science-fiction title for a music that was actually about feeling.

A soul tempo, sped up

Hear it as an album and the length stops being a problem. Seventy-eight minutes of breaks can exhaust a listener who wants a compilation’s variety of producers. One producer, one palette, and the length becomes a climate. Tracks breathe. A vocal line returns in a later piece like a character. The title promises secrets and technology. The music delivers both, and then delivers melody, which is the less advertised ingredient. There is no attempt to sound like a pop crossover with the breaks sanded down. There is also no attempt to prove hardness by removing the singer. The balance is the achievement.

Voodoo Ray had already attached his name to a rave hit. Black Secret Technology does not retell that hit. It assumes you know the producer and then shows you the album he wanted once the breaks had learned to sing. The earlier shock is a door. This is the room.

What the record changed is the idea that jungle’s album form had to wait for a prize. New Forms, two years later, would put drum and bass in a formal gown and take it to the Mercury. Black Secret Technology is earlier, stranger, and more purely Gerald’s. It showed that a single author could hold a CD of this music together, with soul singing as structure rather than as a hook rented for the radio edit. The dance chart’s number 8 is the commercial footprint. The artistic footprint is every later drum-and-bass album that lets a voice finish a sentence.

Five years from now the breaks will still be fast and the singing will still be the reason to stay. Play it after a set of 1994 darkside 12-inches and you will hear what he added: air, tune, a person. Play it before the more famous long jungle albums and you will hear the permission. Juice Box released it in March 1995, 77 minutes and 57 seconds, from the Machine Room. The secret in the title is not a code. It is the decision to make jungle as long, and as sung, as a soul record.
