Get Ready!

2 Unlimited’s debut, released 24 February 1992 on Byte in Antwerp, turned a Belgian instrumental and two Dutch voices into the loudest invitation in eurodance.

An instrumental that grew a duo

Get Ready! is the debut album by 2 Unlimited, released on 24 February 1992. Byte Records in Belgium put it out and licensed it widely: PWL Continental in Britain, ZYX in Germany, Switzerland, and Austria, and other labels beyond. The group is Belgian-Dutch. Jean-Paul De Coster and Phil Wilde, producers in Antwerp, founded it in 1991 as a studio idea. Dutch rapper Ray Slijngaard and Dutch singer Anita Doth became its face. The album runs 59 minutes and 48 seconds. The genres on the sleeve of history are eurodance, techno, and dance-pop. The sound is a kick that does not negotiate, a rap verse, a sung chorus, and a rave stab.

Get Ready for This began as an instrumental in May 1991. De Coster and Wilde wanted a rap on it. Slijngaard, who had already worked with them, returned the tape with his verse and with Doth’s vocal. She was a friend from Amsterdam nightlife, a traffic warden, and a singer. The 12-inch became a club hit. The single then became a chart hit: number 8 in Belgium, number 10 in the Netherlands, number 2 in Australia, number 2 in Britain. Three years after the original release it reached number 38 on the US Hot 100. Twilight Zone followed in January 1992 and topped the Dutch chart, reached number 2 in Britain, and in the United States made number 49 on the Hot 100 and number 5 on the dance chart. The album arrived in February to hold those two singles and seven newer tracks, among them two ballads. Workaholic came out on 20 April 1992. The Magic Friend on 3 August 1992.

The hall, and the ballad

The sound of the hits is efficient on purpose. Get Ready for This is an invitation with the fat trimmed off. The rap sets a scene in a few lines. The hook is a command. The kick is four-on-the-floor at a tempo built for a hall, a fairground, a sports arena. Twilight Zone is a shade darker, a title borrowed from television and a track that still wants every person in the room to move at once. Doth’s voice is the melody. Slijngaard’s is the push. Eurodance as a form can be caricatured as anonymous stabs and a shouted “yeah.” This record has authors. The ballads on the album are the evidence. A project that started as an instrumental needed singers, and the singers could sing when the kick stepped back. The album is not only the two songs a stadium still plays. It is a debut that shows the voices were real.

The chart for the album is more modest than the chart for the singles, which is the right proportion. The Netherlands put it at number 12, Australia at number 10, Britain at number 37, the Billboard 200 at number 197. Australia certified it gold. The United States certified it gold in 1995, at 500,000 copies, long after the clubs had finished with it. Several of the act’s tracks became themes in the NBA and the NHL. A Belgian studio record, fronted by a Dutch rapper and a Dutch singer, ended up as the noise of American sports. That crossing is the change. Eurodance had a face, and the face was not a British rave act or a Detroit producer. It was a duo you could book, film, and shout along with.

The act went on to No Limit and to eighteen million records across a career. Get Ready! is the first statement, and the one with the cleanest invitation. Five years from now Get Ready for This will still be played in rooms that do not know they are playing a 1992 album track. That is a kind of survival. The better way to hear it is as the album: the instrumental that grew a rapper, the rapper who brought a singer, the singer who gave the kick a tune, the ballads that prove the tune was theirs. Antwerp built it. Amsterdam sang it. The world shouted the hook. The record is still the shortest distance between a drum machine and a crowd.
