Summer Madness

Summer Madness is L.T. Drifta’s CC006, an eight-track album on Club Copy, digital, $9 CAD, also marked VCR026D2 on the artist page. The label’s description is the warmest sentence it has written about him: “Warm room. Nobody wants to leave. Vancouver late hours with jazz and soul in the flips.” Released 14 November 2025. Produced for Club Copy and Copy House Publishing. The genre line on the page is electronic, not jungle and not jazz, even though jazz is the word the copy reaches for. That mismatch is the album’s actual subject.

The running order, from the Bandcamp sleeve at ltdrifta.bandcamp.com/album/summer-madness, is “Space and Time” (2:07), “Go!” (3:37), “Summer Madness” (3:20), “Too Far” (2:55), “Nu Saigon” (1:00), “You’re My” (2:51), “So What” (1:43), and “Mood Indigo” (3:02). Added up, that is 20:35. The Club Copy page prints 20:41 and times a few tracks a second longer. The difference is small and the principle is not: when the two sleeves disagree, this piece follows Bandcamp, because that is the public object with the artist’s name on the account. Nothing on the album, by either count, runs to four minutes. “Nu Saigon” is exactly a minute. An album that brief is a decision about attention. It assumes you will notice a short track, and that you do not need a ten-minute version to believe the idea was finished.

A minute-long piece inside an album asks a different question than a minute-long sketch on a beat tape. On a tape, the short track is often an apology or a loop that escaped. Sequenced as track five of eight, between “Too Far” and “You’re My,” “Nu Saigon” is part of the form. The album is built like a set of postcards rather than a novel: each title gets a scene, and the scene ends. Listeners who came up on long club sides sometimes hear brevity as a lack of commitment. The other reading is available, and it fits the rest of his Club Copy run. Classic Jazz also keeps several pieces short. Lakeshore West 23:55 follows a 3:55 title track with “Hold U” at 1:11. The long single, Three 4 Zone, comes later. In November 2025 the method was compression.

The jazz and soul the cover copy mentions show up in the titles, and the titles are as far as a responsible review can go. “Summer Madness” is the name of a 1974 Kool & the Gang instrumental, one of those records that circulated for decades as a mood rather than as a song with a plot. “So What” is the opening track of Miles Davis’s Kind of Blue. “Mood Indigo” is Duke Ellington’s. Whether these eight tracks use those recordings, replay them, or only borrow the names is not stated on the page or on Bandcamp. This magazine will not guess. What it will say is that naming an electronic album’s tracks after a funk instrumental, a modal-jazz standard, and an Ellington ballad, in a month when the same producer had just released Classic Jazz, is a pattern. The pattern is the canon as a set of titles a beatmaker keeps in reach. Homage, source, and coincidence are three different acts. The sleeve does not choose among them. The repetition across two albums makes “coincidence” the least satisfying of the three, without proving either of the others.

“Vancouver late hours” is the one place-name in the copy, and it is worth reading against the biography. L.T. Drifta’s artist page and the label’s Bandcamp both put him in Cumberland, a town on the east coast of Vancouver Island. Vancouver, in the album text, is not offered as his address. It is offered as a time of night: late, warm, nobody leaving. A non-DJ can follow that image without a scene report. It is the hour after the plans, when the music in the room is supposed to behave like weather. The critical risk in the sentence is that “warm” and “soul” can become a compliment that replaces listening. Eight tracks in twenty-odd minutes do not give a writer much camouflage. Either the sequence holds — the dash of “Go!,” the full stop of “Nu Saigon,” the Ellington title last — or the warmth is only advertising.

Set beside CC001, this is the record on which the label stops describing a single session and tries to describe an evening. Champion Sound is one room, quiet on purpose. Summer Madness is a run of rooms, all of them short, with the standards leaning in at the edges and never introduced. That is a more ambitious object than the copy’s coziness suggests. Cozy is easy to sell and hard to finish eight times without a track that overstays. On the timings, nothing overstays. The album’s claim is that a late hour can be edited, and that jazz in the titles is enough jazz to declare. The second claim is the one a listener should test. The first is already on the sleeve.
