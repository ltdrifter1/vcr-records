Snivilisation

Orbital’s third album, released by FFRR on 8 August 1994, is 75 minutes of British techno: acid, landscape, and a duo who had decided an album could be the rave.

The third one

Snivilisation is the third studio album by Orbital, Paul and Phil Hartnoll. FFRR released it in Britain on 8 August 1994. It runs 75 minutes and 4 seconds. They recorded it across 1993 and 1994. The music is techno and ambient techno, which in their hands means long tracks, acid lines, breaks, spoken fragments, and a sense that a piece can change its mind halfway through and still be the same piece. The previous album, the brown-sleeved Orbital, had come out in 1993 and established them as an albums group rather than as a rave act who happened to fill a CD. Snivilisation is the next claim, and it is a darker, funnier, more unsettled claim. The title is a sneer and a diagnosis. Civilisation, snivelling. The music is more generous than the sneer.

Orbital’s method by 1994 was already a public method. Two brothers, sequencers, a live show built from the same machines, tracks that a DJ could play and that a listener could also sit with. The green album in 1991, with Chime, had been the arrival. The brown album had been the proof they could do it again at a higher resolution. Snivilisation assumes that proof and spends its length on character. Forever opens the curtain. I Wish I Had Duck Feet is the joke with a serious rhythm under it, a title no one trying to be severe would allow, and a track that earns the silliness by being tightly made. Sad But True is the emotional one, a melody that aches without borrowing a ballad’s structure. Crash and Carry and Science Friction keep the pace of a night that has decided not to peak once and go home. Philosophy by Numbers and Kein Trink Wasser are the duo at their most architectural, acid and sequence, titles that treat ideas and languages as fair game for a dance record. Quality Seconds, Are We Here?, and Attached close the hour and a quarter. Are We Here? is the question the album is calm enough to ask out loud.

Landscape, not a poster

The sound is wide. A 303 line can be the lead voice for a minute and then step back for a chord that feels like weather over a field. Breaks arrive when the track wants a body, not when a formula demands a drop at the two-minute mark. Spoken fragments, the duo’s habit, work as signs rather than as lyrics you are meant to agree with. There is humor in the edits and melancholy in the harmony, often in the same track. That combination is Orbital’s particular gift, and it is fully grown here. You can dance. You can also notice that the dance is happening in a landscape, not in a poster of a landscape. The environmental feeling is harmonic. It is not a sample of a bird glued to a kick. The kick, when it is there, is techno’s kick, honest and physical. The air around it is the composition.

Seventy-five minutes is the right length for this argument and a dangerous length for a lesser one. A techno album that long has to keep changing or it becomes a compilation of the same peak. Snivilisation changes. The silly title sits beside the sad one. The acid workout sits beside the question. Nothing on it is a pop single in the sense of a three-minute confession, and nothing on it is ambient wallpaper. The Hartnolls were making music for rooms and for headphones at the same time, which was the British electronic album’s best idea in that decade. The idea has a history after this record. In Sides, in 1996, is a different album, with its own studio story and its own singles. Snivilisation does not need that later chapter to be complete. Heard in August order, it is the mid-career statement: the duo past the debut shock, not yet the festival institution, busy with the tracks.

What it changed is the seriousness with which a rave duo could be heard as authors. FFRR, a major’s dance label, released 75 minutes that do not apologise for being techno. The chart was not the point. The point was that a listener could put the CD on at the start of a journey and get off at the end having heard a mind change subject several times. Producers who later made long electronic albums, with section changes and jokes in the titles, were working in a space the Hartnolls had made ordinary. Ordinary is the victory. In 1991 a techno album was still a surprise. In August 1994, from this duo, it was a craft.

Five years from now the titles will still look like a private game, and the music will still explain the game. Play Sad But True if you want the feeling in one corridor. Play Kein Trink Wasser if you want the acid as architecture. Play Are We Here? if you want the question. Play the album if you want the civilisation they were snivelling at, answered not with a slogan but with 75 minutes and 4 seconds of sequence, harmony, and kick. Paul and Phil Hartnoll, FFRR, 8 August 1994. The record is long because the night was long. It still is.

A last practical note, which is also a critical one. Snivilisation rewards being heard in order, at a volume where the low end exists and the small spoken pieces are still intelligible. Shuffle it and you get a series of good techno tracks. Play it as they sequenced it and you get the third Orbital album, which is a better thing: a duo’s idea of how long a thought can be, and of how funny a thought can be, without letting go of the dance. The snarl in the title has aged into a period piece. The sequences have not. Acid lines do not wrinkle when they are this well voiced. A chord that arrives after four minutes of rhythm still arrives. That delay is the craft, and it is the reason the album is worth the time it asks. August 1994 was full of shorter thrills. This is the long one that still pays.
