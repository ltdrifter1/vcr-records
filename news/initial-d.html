Initial D and the Sound of Speed

Eurobeat, tofu runs, and a music scene built on RPMs.

Label noteJul 10, 2023

CJ Archer

Eurobeat, tofu runs, and a music scene built on RPMs.

A manga about a kid delivering tofu down a mountain at 4am shouldn't have changed how a generation heard music. But Initial D did. Shuichi Shigeno's story of an AE86 running mountain passes in Gunma turned into an anime that traveled way past car culture, and it took a whole sound with it. Eurobeat — fast, cheesy, gloriously overproduced Italo-descended dance music nobody in North America was supposed to hear — became the default soundtrack for anyone who ever pointed a controller at a PlayStation or a browser at a fansub site in the early 2000s.

The music was doing something specific. Eurobeat runs at a tempo built for tension, not relaxation — it's the sound of a corner coming up faster than you'd like. That's not an accident. Drifting is controlled loss of control, and the music mirrored it: a beat that never quite resolves, always pushing toward the next apex. Anyone who's spent time in jungle, hardcore, or breaks knows that same trick. Speed isn't just a BPM number. It's a feeling you build into the arrangement on purpose.

The cultural fallout was real, not just aesthetic. Initial D is one of the reasons an AE86 Trueno went from "used Toyota nobody wanted" to a genuine collector's item. It's a big part of why drifting crossed over from a niche Japanese motorsport into something with real events, real sponsors, and real crowds in North America by the mid-2000s. A cartoon about a delivery boy quietly helped build an entire aftermarket industry, one poster on one teenager's wall at a time.

We think about that a lot when we're mixing down anything with real low-end urgency to it — the records built to move a room the way a good drift moves a car: on the edge, on purpose, never fully in control. Eurobeat never made it onto a Club Copy release, but the logic behind it is all over our catalog.
