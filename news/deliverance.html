Deliverance

Baby D’s only album, released 8 February 1996 on Systematic, put a UK number-one jungle song and its singer’s name on a full-length that reached the top five.

Named for the voice

Deliverance is the only album by Baby D, the London breakbeat-hardcore group, released by Systematic on 8 February 1996. It is house, jungle, rave, and pop, in the proportion that British dance used in the middle of the decade when those words still shared a chart. The group took its name from its singer. Caroline Sullivan, reviewing the album in the Guardian on 9 February 1996, said the decision was the right one: dance music has a habit of hiding the women who sing it, and here the voice is the reason the tunes can live on the radio. Music & Media described three musicians from London who had already ruled specialist dance charts and then crossed. The album reached number 5 in Britain and number 47 in Switzerland.

The singles are the plot. Let Me Be Your Fantasy, a 1994 number one, is on the album, seven minutes and fifty seconds of plea over a break. (Everybody’s Got to Learn Sometime) I Need Your Loving and So Pure both reached number 3. Destiny had been a number 69. A live Casanova had been a number 67. Take Me to Heaven, the 1996 single, reached number 15. A limited edition added a second disc: another studio track, a remix by Acen, and further versions. The album is a hit collection that is also a real sequence, twelve songs, not a CD single with the artwork blown up.

Breaks, and a chorus

The sound is the sound of jungle when it still wanted a song. The breaks are fast. The bass is rave bass, rounded enough for a radio, rude enough for a pirate. The vocals are tuned, melodic, and forward in the mix, which is the whole argument with a harder strain of the music that preferred the sample to the singer. Fantasy is the masterpiece of that argument. It is long, it is direct, and it treats a love lyric as something a breakbeat can carry without smirking. So Pure runs seven minutes and does not apologise for the length. I Need Your Loving takes a song people already knew, the Korgis’ soft-rock sigh, and rebuilds it as dance music. That move, a familiar melody on a new rhythm, was one of the decade’s standard magics. Baby D do it without burying the tune.

Taylor Parkes, in Melody Maker on 10 February 1996, heard twelve graceful modern pop songs and refused the idea that pop had to be treated as an heirloom. Music Week had already given the record five out of five at the end of January, citing the trippy textures, the blips, the melodies, and the polished voice that had produced three huge singles. The praise is of its time, and it has stayed accurate. Deliverance is not a difficult album. It is an accomplished one. The accomplishment is a woman’s voice given top billing in a scene that often printed the producer’s name larger, and a set of breaks that can still move a room.

What it changed is small and real. A hardcore act with a number-one single made an album, charted it at number 5, and stopped. There is no second Baby D album to dilute this one. The catalogue is a single object, plus the 12-inches that fed it. Five years from now Fantasy will still be the song people mean, and the album will still be the right way to hear it: not as a novelty week at number one, but as one track among twelve in which the same voice tries other weathers. Euphoria, Nature’s Warning, Take Me to Heaven. The titles are earnest. The rhythms are not naive. British jungle’s pop wing has rarely been this complete, or this willing to put the singer’s name on the cover and mean it.
