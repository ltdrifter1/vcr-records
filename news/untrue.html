Essential Listening: Untrue

Burial's second album, Hyperdub, 5 November 2007: garage slowed down until the walk home is the song.

format
Review
date
Mar 22, 2021
author
CJ Archer
artists
Burial
words
765 words · 3 min
photo
Photo: W.carter

Untrue is Burial’s second album, released on Hyperdub on 5 November 2007, a year after the self-titled debut that had already made a faceless South London producer the most argued-about new name in British dance music. William Bevan’s identity was not public yet. The records arrived as sleeves, a few interviews conducted by email, and a sound that listeners tried to file under dubstep because that was the word the year had available. The filing was never quite right, which is why the album is still the one to hand someone who thinks dubstep means a festival drop.

What a non-specialist needs is a plain description of the materials. The rhythms come from UK garage, the 2-step pattern that dominated British clubs and pirate radio at the end of the 1990s: a kick that refuses a four-on-the-floor march, a shuffle, a skip. Burial drags that pattern down in tempo and leaves the crackle of vinyl, or of a cheap recording, sitting on top of it as if the record were already used. The voices are samples, pitched and cut until they sound remembered rather than performed. Nobody is credited as a singer in the way a pop album credits a singer. The voice is a material, like the hiss.

The tracklist is the argument. “Archangel,” “Near Dark,” “Ghost Hardware,” “Etched Headplate,” “In McDonalds,” “Etched Headplate”’s neighbors “Shell of Light” and “Raver,” the title track. “In McDonalds” is the title that gives the geography away without a postcard. This is not nightclub music in the sense of a room with a lighting rig. It is the music of the time after the room, named for ordinary places, built from a dance rhythm that has lost the crowd and kept the drum. People called the crackle atmosphere. Atmosphere is what you say when you do not want to admit that the distortion is composition. He chose it. It is not the residue of a bad transfer. Or if it is residue, it is residue he refused to clean, which is the same decision.

The debut had already established the palette. Untrue is the one that made the palette feel like a world rather than a single idea repeated. Songs arrive with something closer to a chorus — a vocal phrase that returns — without ever becoming songs you could hand to a conventional vocalist. That halfway state is the record’s invention. Garage had been full of vocal tracks, diva edits, hearts on sleeves. Dubstep, in the form that was about to go global, was about to become largely instrumental and very loud. Burial stood in the door between them and did not pick a side. The emotional register is grief, or homesickness, and it is not vague. “Raver” is a title with a thesis: the person who went out is now the subject, past tense built into the noun.

Critical reputation did this album a partial disservice by turning it into a shortcut. For a few years, Untrue was the record writers used to prove that electronic music could be melancholy and serious, as if melancholy were a promotion out of the dance. The drums say otherwise. They are dance drums, from a specific British club lineage, played at a speed you can still move to in a kitchen. The seriousness is not that the album escaped the club. It is that it took the club’s rhythm home and refused to brighten it up for company. Hyperdub, Steve Goodman’s label, was the right door for that refusal. The label’s other releases made a context: this was a scene, not a solitary genius myth, even if the sleeves encouraged the myth by withholding a face.

Heard in 2026, the album’s risk is nostalgia of a second order. Listeners now approach it as a classic, which means they listen for the crackle they already love and miss the severity of the editing. The vocals are not there to be sad on your behalf. They are chopped until a feeling becomes a rhythm. That is closer to hip-hop’s treatment of a sample than to a singer-songwriter’s treatment of a confession, and it is the part that still has work to do. If you want the rain-on-the-glass version, it exists, and it is smaller than the record. The record is a garage album that accepted daylight was not coming and built the songs anyway. South London is in the titles and in the interviews he eventually gave. You do not need our coast to understand it. You need a bus that has stopped running and a drum pattern that has not.

CJ Archer
