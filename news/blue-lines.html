Blue Lines

Massive Attack’s debut album was released on 8 April 1991 by Wild Bunch and Virgin. The singles started the autumn before. Tricky is in the credits. The solo album is four years away.

8 April 1991

Blue Lines is Massive Attack’s debut studio album, released 8 April 1991 on Wild Bunch and Virgin. The length on the Wikipedia page is 45 minutes and 8 seconds. The producers are the group and Jonny Dollar. The members named in the recording credit are Grantley “Daddy G” Marshall, Robert “3D” Del Naja, Adrian “Tricky” Thaws, and Andrew “Mushroom” Vowles. Shara Nelson and Horace Andy sing. The studios are Coach House in Bristol, and Eastcote, Cherry Bear, Abbey Road, and Hot Nights in London. Recording is dated 1990–1991. Forty-five minutes is a short debut beside the doubles that drum and bass would press later in the decade. It is long enough to be an album and short enough to have been sequenced rather than collected.

The singles map a longer public life than the release week. “Daydreaming,” 15 October 1990. “Unfinished Sympathy,” 11 February 1991. The album, 8 April. “Safe from Harm,” 27 May 1991. “Hymn of the Big Wheel” / “Be Thankful for What You’ve Got,” 10 February 1992. Two of those dates are handled in the February volume of this magazine, because that is when those singles came out. The album date is April, so the album is here. A reader who meets only this page should still see the order. The group did not appear, complete, on 8 April. They had been issuing songs since the previous autumn. The album is the sequence that gathered the songs and the ones that had not been singles yet.

Bristol and the other rooms

The city in the legend is Bristol. The studio list supports Bristol and then spends four names on London, including Abbey Road. A debut made in both places is a debut that used the city it is identified with and the facilities a label books when the album has to be finished. Wild Bunch on the label line is the collective’s name carried onto the record company credit. Virgin is the major. The same double structure shows up on other records in this run — Hard Hands with Columbia, Juice Box alone, Warp alone — and it is worth seeing the versions side by side. Blue Lines is the version where the collective’s name and the major’s name share the label line in 1991, before “trip hop” was a bin people trusted.

The page calls the album, in the general account, the first trip-hop album, a blend of hip-hop’s breakbeats, sampling, and rapping with dub, soul, reggae, and electronic music. “Generally regarded” is the page’s own caution. This magazine will keep the caution. Firsts are usually a fight about definitions. What is safer, and more useful, is the blend as a description of method: rapping and singing on the same record, breaks and dub bass, samples, a British group, a short running time. Tricky’s name in the member list is the fact that connects this April to his February 1995 debut, Maxinquaye, without making the later album a sequel. He left enough room to make a solo record. He is still in these credits. Both statements are true because they refer to different years.

What the length refuses

Forty-five minutes means the album ends. It does not drift until the CD is full, which was already possible in 1991. A group often praised for atmosphere made a debut that fits on one side of a C90 with room for the singles’ radio edits and still comes home under three-quarters of an hour. Atmosphere, on this evidence, was not an excuse for duration. The long life of individual songs — a single in February, another almost a year after the album — happened outside the album’s length. Inside it, the songs had to live with each other in a space that could be played after dinner and still be over before the night required a second record. That is a sequencing virtue. It is measurable.

Jonny Dollar’s co-production credit keeps the album from being a story of four people only. A named co-producer is a fifth pair of hands, and the page puts him on the producer line with the group. Shara Nelson’s and Horace Andy’s vocal credits keep it from being a story of rappers only. The debut is a singers’ record and a rap record and a production record. Later arguments about which of those it “really” was are arguments about emphasis. The credit block does not choose. April 1991 is when that block became an album a shop could file under Wild Bunch and Virgin.

The criticism worth keeping is against two reductions. One reduction makes Blue Lines the invention of a genre and then stops listening. The other makes it a Bristol postcard and leaves Abbey Road out of the studio list. The record is a 45-minute debut with a single already in the world, a major on the label line, a co-producer, two singers, and a member who would release a solo album on 20 February 1995. Those are enough facts to file it under April and to leave the genre fight in the “generally regarded” clause where the encyclopedia left it.

Written as a strip, the file is short enough to check. Date, 8 April 1991. Labels, Wild Bunch and Virgin. Length, 45:08. Producers, the group and Jonny Dollar. Members, Daddy G, 3D, Tricky, Mushroom. Vocals, Shara Nelson and Horace Andy. Studios, Coach House in Bristol, then Eastcote, Cherry Bear, Abbey Road, and Hot Nights in London. Singles, in the order already given: 15 October 1990, 11 February 1991, 27 May 1991, 10 February 1992. The solo record that takes one of those members out of this credit block is Maxinquaye, 20 February 1995, filed in the February volume. Nothing in the strip requires a night out to verify. The songs were made for nights. The strip is what the page can still prove, which is why a feature about a debut can end on a list and still be about the music. The music is the list’s reason for existing. The list is how a later reader knows which object was being discussed.
