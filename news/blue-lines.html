Blue Lines

Massive Attack’s debut, released 8 April 1991 on Wild Bunch and Virgin, built a British album out of breaks, dub, soul singing, and rap, and gave it time.

A slow ensemble

Blue Lines is the debut album by Massive Attack, released on 8 April 1991 by Wild Bunch Records and Virgin. It runs 45 minutes and 8 seconds. The producers are the group and Jonny Dollar. The group, in that chapter, is Grant Marshall, known as Daddy G; Robert Del Naja, known as 3D; Adrian Thaws, known as Tricky; and Andrew Vowles, known as Mushroom. Shara Nelson sings. Horace Andy sings. They recorded at the Coach House in Bristol and at Eastcote, Cherry Bear, Abbey Road, and Hot Nights in London. The music is what happens when hip-hop’s breaks, dub’s space, soul’s melodies, and rap’s close detail agree to share a slow tempo.

The singles tell you the album was assembled in public. Daydreaming came out on 15 October 1990. Unfinished Sympathy on 11 February 1991. Safe from Harm on 27 May 1991. Hymn of the Big Wheel, paired with a version of Be Thankful for What You’ve Got, on 10 February 1992. So the record has a before and an after. It is not a sudden object. It is the sequence that made those songs face one another. Forty-five minutes is short beside the double albums that later claimed this territory. It is long enough. Nothing on it hurries to justify its place.

Strings, a break, a voice

Unfinished Sympathy is the piece that taught the widest audience what the group could do. A breakbeat, unshowy and persistent. A bass that walks rather than drops. And then strings, a real arrangement, under Shara Nelson’s voice, which is adult, aching, and in no mood to be a sample. The song is about a sympathy that cannot be completed, and the arrangement agrees: it builds, it crests, it does not resolve into a grin. Safe from Harm is the other Nelson single, a warning with a groove, her voice in front of a harder drum, the group rapping in the margins like men who know the singer is carrying the feeling. Daydreaming is lighter and still slow, a summer inside the same palette. Hymn of the Big Wheel closes the story with Horace Andy, the reggae singer whose tone is high, weathered, and calm. A Bristol crew ending an album on a Jamaican voice and a hymn is the mix of the city, stated without a speech.

The rapping matters, and it is easy to under-describe because the singing is so strong. 3D’s voice is dry, observational, a man making notes. Tricky’s is already the mutter he would later expand on his own records, a presence at the edge of the mix. Daddy G is warmer. They do not trade bars like a New York crew trying to win a round. They place lines the way the samples are placed: for climate. The breaks are head-nod breaks, the sort a hip-hop producer would have used for a verse, here used as the bed of a song that also has a string line. Dub is the space between those elements. You can hear the room. You can hear decisions about what not to add.

What Blue Lines changed is the British possibility of that blend. A group from Bristol, on a label named for the sound-system crew they had come out of, made an album the major could release without sanding it into pop-rap or into indie rock. The records that followed in that city, by these people and by others, inherited the tempo and the seriousness. The shop name trip-hop arrived later, and it has always been too cute for the music. The music is slower than that name. It is a debut that already sounds like a group who have decided what to leave out.

Five years from now Unfinished Sympathy will still be the song, and the album will still be the reason the song is not a one-off. Play it in order. Safe from Harm’s warning, the hymn at the end, Nelson and Andy as two kinds of singer the breaks are willing to serve. Forty-five minutes, 8 April 1991, Bristol and London studios, a crew and a producer named Dollar. The lines in the title are telephone lines, bass lines, and the lines of a city after dark. All three are still audible. The record does not need a revival. It needs a volume knob and a listener who will let a break breathe.
