Her voice, his weather

Maxinquaye is Tricky’s debut album, released on 20 February 1995 by 4th & Broadway and Island. The catalogue number on the vinyl is BRLP 610. He had signed in 1993. He recorded the album in 1994, most of it in a home studio in London. Martina Topley-Bird is the main singer. Alison Goldfrapp, Ragga, and Mark Stewart appear as well. The name on the sleeve is his. The sound of the record is theirs, and mostly hers and his, a conversation in which one person almost whispers and the other sings in a clear, young, unhurried voice.

Tricky had already been a voice inside Massive Attack’s world. Maxinquaye is his own sequence. It takes the Bristol habit of slowing hip-hop and dub until a song can sit in the smoke, and it makes that habit private. The drums are low and slightly late. The bass is a presence more than a riff. Samples feel second-hand in the best way, as if the record were remembering other records through a wall. Over that, Topley-Bird sounds exposed and calm. She is not decorating a rap album. She is half the composition. His voice, when it enters, is a mutter beside her, close to the microphone, suspicious of melody, full of it anyway.

A cover, and a room

Black Steel is the moment the method shows its nerve. Public Enemy’s Black Steel in the Hour of Chaos is a sprint and a manifesto, Chuck D in command, the Bomb Squad in attack. Tricky and Topley-Bird turn it into a murmur. The lyric survives. The posture does not. A song about a man refusing the draft becomes a song sung by a woman in a small room, and the refusal gets stranger, more intimate, no less serious. That choice is the album in miniature. Take a piece of hip-hop authority and move it into a register where authority has to speak softly.

The rest of the record stays in that register. Overcome opens the door with a drag in the rhythm and a vocal that seems to lean on the beat rather than ride it. Aftermath is dread with a tune. Hell Is Round the Corner walks a bassline that feels borrowed from soul and then locked in a smaller room. Ponderosa and Suffocated Love are titles that do not oversell the mood. The production keeps finding air inside the claustrophobia: a guitar figure, a sudden space, a bar in which the drums step back and her voice is the only loud thing. Mark Stewart’s presence connects the music to an older Bristol avant-garde, the Pop Group’s splinters. Goldfrapp’s voice, before her own electronic career, is another color in a palette that is mostly grey-green and close.

What Maxinquaye changed is the second path of that Bristol slowing. Blue Lines had shown that a crew could build songs from dub, soul, breaks, and rapping. This album showed that one of those voices could leave and make a whole world with a singer the mainstream had not met. Trip-hop, as a shop name, came later and got applied backwards. The music was already here: hip-hop’s drums at a walking pace, a woman singing, a man half in shadow, samples used for climate rather than for a punchline. The home studio is audible in the best sense. The record feels near. You can imagine the lamp.

It is worth hearing in five years because the climate has not been improved upon by louder equipment. A modern imitation can buy the same smoke and still miss the balance, which is Topley-Bird’s tone against his mutter, neither of them winning. Play Black Steel after the Public Enemy original and the distance is the art. Play the album through, in a room, without skipping to the familiar titles. The sequencing is a descent that never quite agrees to be depressing. There is groove in it. There is wit. There is a debut that already sounds like a last record, and then refuses to end that way. Island and 4th & Broadway put a home recording into the world on 20 February 1995. The room is still small. That is why it still fits.
