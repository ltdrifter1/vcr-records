Maxinquaye

Tricky’s debut album was released on 20 February 1995 by 4th & B’way. Martina Topley-Bird sings most of it. The record is not a Massive Attack album with one member stepped forward.

20 February 1995

Maxinquaye is Tricky’s debut studio album, released on 20 February 1995 by 4th & B’way, a subsidiary of Island. That date is on his own site and on the Wikipedia page for the album. Discogs lists a European vinyl the same day: 4th & Broadway BRLP 610, also numbered Island 524 089-1, filed under electronic, with the style tags leftfield, downtempo, and trip hop. The genre tags are the after-the-fact shelf. The date and the catalogue number are the object.

Wikipedia’s account of how the record came to exist is specific enough to use and narrow enough to trust as a summary of the public story. Tricky, Adrian Thaws, had a limited role in Massive Attack and wanted a project of his own. He signed a solo contract with 4th & B’way in 1993. He recorded the album the next year, primarily at a home studio in London. Martina Topley-Bird is the main vocalist. Alison Goldfrapp, Ragga, and Mark Stewart are credited with additional vocals. Those are the names. A debut that is mostly sung by someone who is not the name on the sleeve is a structural fact, not a footnote. The sleeve says Tricky. The lead voice, on the page’s own wording, is Topley-Bird’s.

Not a chapter of Blue Lines

Massive Attack’s Blue Lines was released on 8 April 1991. Tricky is part of that group’s story, and the Wikipedia summary of Maxinquaye says the solo album came out of frustration with the size of his role there. The frustration is the motive the encyclopedia reports. It is not a reason to file the 1995 album as a lost Massive Attack session. The label is different. The year is different. The studio, a home setup in London rather than the Coach House in Bristol and the London rooms Blue Lines used, is different. The vocalist who carries it is different. Influence is not the same thing as authorship. A magazine that also keeps a piece on Blue Lines, in a later volume, can say they share a city and a former colleague without pretending they are one catalogue.

The tracklist on Tricky’s site gives durations that already describe a record with no interest in hiding its length: “Overcome” 4:30, “Ponderosa” 3:30, “Black Steel” 5:39, “Hell Is Round the Corner” 3:47, “Pumpkin” 4:30, “Aftermath,” and the songs after them. “Black Steel” is a version of a Public Enemy song. That fact is widely printed and this piece will not invent a comparison beyond it. A cover is a decision to put someone else’s composition inside your album and let the arrangement take the blame or the credit. Here the arrangement is the debut’s. The song is not.

What February was also doing

Maxinquaye did not have the month to itself. Nine days earlier, on 7 February 1995, Mobb Deep released the single “Shook Ones, Part II,” ahead of The Infamous in April. Ten days before that single, Leftfield had released Leftism, on 30 January. A week after Maxinquaye’s calendar neighbours, nothing in this paragraph needs a theory of a scene. The dates are simply close. Bristol and London and New York were putting out records in the same winter without sharing a label or a tempo. Trip hop, the tag Discogs reaches for, is one of the names applied afterward to some of the British records and not to the Queens album. The name is a bin. The February calendar is a better index. It shows a debut, a single from a different tradition, and an album that had landed at the end of January, all inside a few weeks, none of them the explanation of the others.

Home studio is the phrase Wikipedia uses, and it should be left as a phrase about place and means, not turned into a romance of poverty. A home studio in London in 1994 could be modest or it could be a professional room that happened to be in a house. The page does not itemise the gear. What it establishes is that the album was not made at the rooms named on Blue Lines. Independence, on this record, is a contract with 4th & B’way in 1993 and a recording the following year. The romance can be retired. The contract date is more interesting. He had the solo deal two years before the album. The album is not a sudden exit. It is a record delivered on a deal that already existed.

Topley-Bird’s position is the fact a later listener is most likely to get wrong, because the authorship convention of the time put one name on the spine. The public credits do not. Main vocalist is not “featured.” Additional vocals are marked separately, which is how you know the main credit is doing work. A review that describes the album as Tricky’s voice throughout is not reading the page. His voice is on it. It is not the voice the credit list calls the main one. That distinction changes how the record should be shelved in a magazine: as a production and a writing project whose principal singer is named Martina Topley-Bird.

Twenty February 1995 is early enough in the year that a volume for February can hold it without borrowing atmosphere from spring. The record does not need the rest of 1995 to justify itself. It needs its date, its label, its singer, and the refusal to collapse it back into the group he had just made a point of leaving room from. Massive Attack continued. Tricky continued. The album is the document of the gap between those two facts, recorded in a home studio and issued on 4th & B’way with a catalogue number a shop could order.
