Illmatic

Nas released his debut on 19 April 1994 through Columbia. The producers are DJ Premier, Large Professor, Pete Rock, Q-Tip, L.E.S., Faith N., and Nas. The beats are the part a magazine of instrumental culture has to name.

Illmatic came out on 19 April 1994, Columbia, recorded in 1992 and 1993 at Battery, Unique, Chung King, and D&D in New York. The length is 39 minutes and 48 seconds. It debuted at number 12 on the Billboard 200, selling 59,000 copies in the first week, a figure the Wikipedia page says fell below expectations. It was certified gold by the RIAA on 17 January 1996 and platinum on 11 December 2001. As of 20 April 2014 the page’s sales figure for the United States is 1,686,000. A tenth-anniversary edition followed on 30 March 2004. The commercial story is a slow one: a modest first week, a gold plaque almost two years later, a platinum plaque in the next century, a back-catalogue number that took twenty years.

The producer list is the reason the album sits in a volume that also cares about instrumental hip-hop. DJ Premier, Large Professor, Pete Rock, Q-Tip, L.E.S., Faith N., and Nas himself are the names on the production line. This is a rap album. The voice is the point of the debut, written, the page says, from age sixteen, about Queensbridge. Pretending the vocals are incidental would be a lie told for the sake of a category. The category still has a job. The people who made the beats on this record are the same names instrumental hip-hop treats as a canon of their own: Premier’s loops, Pete Rock’s soul chops, Large Professor’s drums, Q-Tip’s ear from A Tribe Called Quest. On Illmatic those practices are under a voice, not instead of one. The culture that later releases the beats alone is studying a record where the beats were never alone. Naming the producers is how you show the study is of a real object.

Thirty-nine minutes is closer to Blue Lines, out the same month three years earlier, than to the long jungle albums of 1994 and 1995. Two short albums in April, years apart, one in Bristol and London and one in Queens, are not a pair. They are two examples of debuts that did not fill a CD to prove they were albums. Illmatic’s shortness was held against it by some listeners at the time, in the form of the disappointing first-week number, and later treated as discipline. The page records both the disappointment and the later stature. A magazine does not have to pick which mood was correct. It has to print the number of minutes and the number of copies.

Bootlegging is part of the release story the page tells. Jeff Weiss is quoted, via the page, on MC Serch finding a garage with 60,000 unofficial copies, regional demand having outrun the label. If that figure is Serch’s claim, it should stay attributed. It explains a debut that the official first week undercounted relative to how widely the music had already moved. April 19 is the legitimate street date. The garages, if the claim holds, were ahead of it. Instrumental culture inherits this problem every time a beat circulates before the album: the object and the official date come apart. Illmatic is an early, documented case, with a producer list long enough to be a scene in itself.
