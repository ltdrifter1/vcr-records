Illmatic

Nas’s debut, released 19 April 1994 on Columbia, is ten songs and under forty minutes: Queensbridge, five producers, and a rapper who arrived finished.

Short, and finished

Illmatic is Nas’s debut album, released by Columbia on 19 April 1994. It runs 39 minutes and 48 seconds. He recorded it in 1992 and 1993 at Battery, Unique, Chung King, and D&D in New York. The producers are DJ Premier, Large Professor, Pete Rock, Q-Tip, L.E.S., and Faith N., with Nas himself in the chair as well. It entered the Billboard 200 at number 12 and sold 59,000 copies in its first week, a modest commercial opening for a record that would become a measuring stick. The RIAA certified it gold on 17 January 1996 and platinum on 11 December 2001. By 20 April 2014, counted US sales stood at 1,686,000. A tenth-anniversary edition followed the original by a decade, on 30 March 2004. The numbers matter less than the length. Under forty minutes, and nothing is missing.

He was writing about Queensbridge Houses, the project in Long Island City where he grew up, and he was young enough that the writing still has the temperature of a person inside the subject. The voice is nasal, precise, and calm even when the content is not. Internal rhymes lock. A verse can carry a scene, a joke, a threat, and a piece of city planning. He does not waste bars on introducing himself at length. The intro does that work with other people’s voices, and then N.Y. State of Mind begins and the album is simply happening. Premier’s beat for that song, a loop that feels like a staircase, is the sound of New York rap in that season: dusty, musical, hard, and short on glossy chorus.

A project, reported

The producer list is a map of the city’s best rooms, and the miracle is that the album sounds like one author. Large Professor’s tracks are soulful and direct. Pete Rock’s The World Is Yours is the wide one, a chorus you can sing, a brass sample that lifts without becoming pop. Q-Tip’s One Love is a letter, quieter, the beat stepping back so the address can land. Premier’s Memory Lane and N.Y. State of Mind are the grim beauty. L.E.S. and the others hold their lanes. Nas’s pen is the unity. Life’s a Bitch, with AZ, is the collaboration that proves he could share a track without losing it. Halftime, already known from a soundtrack, sits in the sequence as a proof of skill. It Ain’t Hard to Tell closes on a Large Professor beat and a rhyme exhibition that still feels like a door shutting on a complete thought.

The sound is jazz and soul samples chopped into beats that knock, and a voice that treats reportage as a lyric art. There is no skit culture, no twenty-minute thank-you, no song built only to feed radio. The hooks, when they exist, are part of the song. The World Is Yours is the one a stranger can hum. The rest ask you to listen to sentences. That demand is why the album has lasted. It is also why the first-week number was 59,000 and not a million. The million came later, as people caught up to a debut that had not waited for them.

What Illmatic changed is the standard for a rap debut. After it, a first album from a New York rapper could be judged on whether it was finished, whether the producers were worthy, whether the writing about a place was specific enough to be believed. Many records have reached for that standard and made longer, louder versions. The original remains short. Five years from now the Queensbridge detail will still be detail, not nostalgia, because the lines are too exact to blur. Play The World Is Yours if you need the lift. Play N.Y. State of Mind if you need the stairs. Play the album through if you want the argument: a kid, a project, five or six of the best beatmakers alive, 39 minutes and 48 seconds, 19 April 1994. Columbia pressed it. The borough has been answering it ever since.
