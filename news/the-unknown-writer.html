DETROIT

The Unknown Writer

Submerge issued Drexciya's The Quest in 1997, on double vinyl and on CD. The sleeve, signed The Unknown Writer, asked whether babies thrown from slave ships could have been born not needing air, and said the questions would not be answered.

In 1997 Submerge, the Detroit company that distributed a large part of the city’s techno, issued Drexciya’s The Quest on double vinyl and on CD. The two-CD edition runs to 28 tracks: the earlier EPs, and a little that was new. The sleeve notes were signed The Unknown Writer. They asked whether a human can breathe underwater, and noted that a foetus is already alive in an aquatic environment. Then the sentence that the record is remembered for. “During the greatest holocaust the world has ever known, pregnant America-bound African slaves were thrown overboard by the thousands during labor for being sick and disruptive cargo. Is it possible that they could have given birth at sea to babies that never needed air?” The notes close on the refusal. “These are many of the questions that you don’t know and never will.”

Before the album, a listener had titles, a few spoken lines, and etchings in the runout. James Stinson and Gerald Donald met at Charles F. Kettering Senior High School in Detroit. Stinson was the older of the two, and for a time a mentor. He told John Osselaer of Techno Tourist, in 2002, that the name arrived on a night he could not sleep. Around 3am on 18 September 1989 he stood up and said “Drexciya.” It felt, he said, like a tidal wave rushing across his brain. The first EP, Deep Sea Dweller, came in 1992, on Shockwave, a sub-label of Underground Resistance. After it: Bubble Metropolis, Molecular Enhancement, The Unknown Aquazone, The Journey Home, and The Return of Drexciya.

A speed, then a date

Bubble Metropolis opens with a voice. “This is Drexciyan Cruiser Control Bubble 1 to Lardossian Cruiser 8-203X, please decrease your speed to 1.788.4 kilobahns.” Wavejumper tells the listener, “You must face the power of the black wave of Lardossa before you become a Drexciyan Wavejumper.” One side of the Deep Sea Dweller 12-inch is etched “TECHNO FROM THE DEEP.” The other side is etched “DEEP H2O.” A speed given to a fictional cruiser, and two phrases cut into the metal, are already a world. They are not yet a history.

The history arrives as a dispatch. On the 1995 EP Aquatic Invasion, Underground Resistance UR-030, the liner notes are credited to The Unknown Writer. “On February First Nineteen Hundred And Ninety Five the Drexciyan Tactical Seaforces received orders from UR Strikeforce Command, for one final mission.” The stingray and barracuda battalions are sent from the Bermuda Triangle. The mission is set for the winter equinox of 1995, against “the programmer strongholds,” with one more blow on the way home to “the invisible city.” The text ends “Aquatic knowledge for those who know.”

Four maps sit with The Quest notes. Each has a title: “The Slave Trade (1655–1857),” “Migration Route of Rural Blacks to Northern Cities (1930s–1940s),” “Techno Leaves Detroit Spreads Worldwide (1988),” and “The Journey Home (Future).” The questions on the sleeve stay open. The maps run in one direction. A trade, a migration, a music leaving a city in a named year, and a return that has not happened. 1988 is printed as the year the music leaves. Detroit is in the middle of the sequence, not at the start of it. The start is the ships.

One take

Stinson told WDET-FM’s Liz Warner, in 2002, “I don’t go out to clubs or anything because I’m dedicated to what I do.” If he went out, he said, he would pick something up, and he did not want it in the music. The dedication is a method. Stay out of the room so the record does not come back from the room.

Kodwo Eshun told a Red Bull Music Academy audience how the two of them started a session. They would imagine a submersible at the bottom of the ocean, pressure on the metal, pitch dark, and then switch on the lights like searchlights. Eshun spent four days with Donald during the 2010 film Hydra Decapita, and was only allowed to record him answering track titles read out loud. The depth they pictured was the Mariana Trench. You agree on where you are, in the dark, under pressure, and then you switch on the lights. The track titles are the report of what the lights found.

The Quest was also a temporary retirement. In 1998 the track “Aquatacizem” appeared on Underground Resistance’s Interstellar Fugitives, with the group described as missing in action. Stinson told Richard Brophy, for the Irish zine Etronik, that he had had health problems and became seriously ill, and that they had to shut things down for a while. They came back in November 1999, when Tresor released Neptune’s Lair, the first official studio album, with “Andreaen Sand Dunes” on it. Tresor followed with 12-inches in 2000 and Harnessed the Storm in 2002. Clone, in the Netherlands, released Grava 4 the same year. The titles turn. “Drexcyen Star Chamber.” “Astronomical Guidepost.” The water gives way to a sky.

On 3 September 2002 Stinson died, at 32, of heart complications. Donald went on under other names. The Drexciya project ended there. Stroiber, who had signed Neptune’s Lair and Harnessed the Storm for Tresor, said the story was not a pitch. Stinson had explained the Drexciyans so the artwork could be made. They were fighting, he told her, for peace and for the good things in the world. The music came out of his stomach and his mind when he went into the studio, and it had to come out. In the Warner interview he describes the making in one sentence that is also a studio report. It came out of him and out of his partner, from the soul, and they dug it out in one take.

The same interview is where he refuses the job of explaining the Storm Series, the run of records under several names in 2001 and 2002. “It’s not for me to say, it’s up to the listener. It’s the same way it’s been from day one with all the Drexciya music.” If a listener does not see what he would have named, the naming would be a mistake. “You listen to it, you tell me: what do you feel?” Pressed for the theme, he answered, “An infinite journey through inner space, the space within and find the beauty inside.” Asked in 2013 about afrofuturism, Donald told Electronic Beats, “I do not wish to specify any particular ethnicity. I would state that all variations of humanity have contributed to the evolution of electronic music.” Neither of them publicly commented on the Quest myth. The sleeve had already asked the questions. The producers did not close them.

A compilation that gathers five years of 12-inches and prints a map is doing two kinds of publishing at once. The tracks are the work that already existed, priced for a while as imports a lot of listeners could not buy. The CD made the music something you could take home. The notes are a different object, bound to that home copy. They ask a question about the Middle Passage and then say the question will not be answered. The maps beside the question are a sequence with years on it, and one of the years is the year a music leaves Detroit. The etch on the first EP had already said the techno was from the deep. The 1997 sleeve asked who lived there, set the ships at the beginning of the route, and left the last map as a direction. Stinson died on 3 September 2002. The questions on the sleeve were already written in the form of questions. Reprinting the records did not turn them into answers.
