The Unknown Writer

Submerge issued Drexciya's The Quest in 1997, on double vinyl and on CD. The sleeve, signed The Unknown Writer, asked whether babies thrown from slave ships could have been born not needing air, and said the questions would not be answered.

In 1997 Submerge, the Detroit company that distributed a large part of the city’s techno, issued Drexciya’s The Quest on double vinyl and on CD. Ben Cardew, writing in DJ Mag on 2 March 2023, calls it a compilation: tracks from the earlier EPs, plus a small amount of new material, and, in the two-CD edition, 28 tracks. The sleeve notes were signed The Unknown Writer. Cardew quotes them. “During the greatest holocaust the world has ever known, pregnant America-bound African slaves were thrown overboard by the thousands during labor for being sick and disruptive cargo. Is it possible that they could have given birth at sea to babies that never needed air?” Marcus Barnes, in Mixmag on 19 October 2020, quotes the question that comes before that one, about whether a human can breathe underwater, and the foetus already alive in an aquatic environment. He also quotes the last line. “These are many of the questions that you don’t know and never will.”

The notes are the first time, in Cardew’s account, that the myth is made explicit. Before the album, a listener had titles, a few spoken lines, and etchings in the runout. Cardew writes that James Stinson and Gerald Donald met at Charles F. Kettering Senior High School in Detroit, Stinson the older of the two and, for a time, a mentor. Stinson told John Osselaer of Techno Tourist, in an interview Cardew cites from 2002, that the name arrived on a night he could not sleep. Around 3am on 18 September 1989 he stood up and said “Drexciya.” It felt, he said, like a tidal wave rushing across his brain. The first EP, Deep Sea Dweller, came in 1992. Barnes adds that it appeared on Shockwave, a sub-label of Underground Resistance. Cardew’s sequence of the 12-inches after it runs Bubble Metropolis, Molecular Enhancement, The Unknown Aquazone, The Journey Home, and The Return of Drexciya.

Kilobahns, then a date

Bubble Metropolis opens, Cardew writes, with a voice: “This is Drexciyan Cruiser Control Bubble 1 to Lardossian Cruiser 8-203X, please decrease your speed to 1.788.4 kilobahns.” Wavejumper tells the listener, “You must face the power of the black wave of Lardossa before you become a Drexciyan Wavejumper.” One side of the Deep Sea Dweller 12-inch is etched “TECHNO FROM THE DEEP.” The other side is etched “DEEP H2O.” A speed given to a fictional cruiser, and two phrases cut into the metal, are already a world. They are not yet a history.

The history arrives as a dispatch. Barnes quotes the text on the 1995 EP Aquatic Invasion, which he attributes to The Unknown Writer, Cornelius Harris, a familiar voice at Underground Resistance. “On February First Nineteen Hundred And Ninety Five the Drexciyan Tactical Seaforces received orders from UR Strikeforce Command, for one final mission.” The stingray and barracuda battalions are sent from the Bermuda Triangle. The mission is set for the winter equinox of 1995, against “the programmer strongholds,” with one more blow on the way home to “the invisible city.” The text ends “Aquatic knowledge for those who know.” Barnes is careful about the authorship: the EP text is Harris’s, and it would at least have been discussed with Stinson and Donald. Cardew, on The Quest two years later, uses a softer verb. The sleeve notes are “widely assumed” to be Harris. The two articles do not contradict each other so much as they keep the assumption visible on the album and firmer on the EP. What both of them print is the writing. A date. A chain of command. A city you cannot see.

Four maps

Barnes describes the maps that sit with The Quest notes. Four of them, simple cartography, each with a title: “The Slave Trade (1655–1857),” “Migration Route of Rural Blacks to Northern Cities (1930s–1940s),” “Techno Leaves Detroit Spreads Worldwide (1988),” and “The Journey Home (Future).” The questions on the sleeve stay open. The maps do not. They run in one direction: a trade, a migration, a music leaving a city in a named year, and a return that has not happened. 1988 is not explained on the map. It is printed as the year the music leaves. Detroit is in the middle of the sequence, not at the start of it. The start is the ships.

Cardew’s description of the music is the other half of the same object, and it should stay his. He calls the classic sound an aqueous electro glide, an 808’s snap against synth lines that suggest a voyage, and he names “Antivapor Waves,” “Bubble Metropolis,” and “Depressurization” as the places a listener can hear the dark and the light together. “You Don’t Know” and “Aqua Jujidsu” are the severe ones. “Dehydration” and “Aquabon (Remix)” are where he hears a theatrical humour. “Intensified Magnetron” and “Aquatic Bata Particles” are the funk. He also records Stinson telling WDET-FM’s Liz Warner, in 2002, “I don’t go out to clubs or anything because I’m dedicated to what I do.” Barnes has a longer cut of the same interview. Stinson says that if he goes out he will pick something up, and he does not want it in the music. The dedication is a method: stay out of the room so the record does not come back from the room.

Kodwo Eshun, the British-Ghanaian writer, told a Red Bull Music Academy audience a version of how the two of them started a session. Cardew’s quotation has them imagining a submersible at the bottom of the ocean, pressure on the metal, pitch dark, then the lights coming on like searchlights. Barnes, who spent the comparison on Eshun’s account of four days with Donald during the 2010 film Hydra Decapita, adds that Donald would only be recorded answering track titles read out to him, and that the depth they pictured was the Mariana Trench. A working rule that specific is not a mood. You agree on where you are, in the dark, under pressure, and then you switch on the lights. The track titles are the report of what the lights found.

One take, then the date

The Quest was also, Cardew writes, a temporary retirement. In 1998 the track “Aquatacizem” appeared on Underground Resistance’s Interstellar Fugitives, with the group described as missing in action. Stinson told Richard Brophy, for the Irish zine Etronik, that he had had health problems and became seriously ill, and that they had to shut things down for a while. The return Cardew dates to November 1999, when Tresor released Neptune’s Lair, which he calls the first official studio album, with “Andreaen Sand Dunes” on it. Tresor followed with 12-inches in 2000 and Harnessed the Storm in 2002. Clone, in the Netherlands, released Grava 4 the same year. Cardew notes the turn in the titles: “Drexcyen Star Chamber,” “Astronomical Guidepost,” the water giving way to a sky. He writes that some listeners took the turn as the end of the quest, or as another phase.

On 3 September 2002 Stinson died. Cardew and Barnes agree on the date and the age, 32. Cardew gives the cause as heart complications. Barnes writes that Donald went on under other names and that the Drexciya project ended there. Stroiber, who had signed Neptune’s Lair and Harnessed the Storm for Tresor, told Barnes the story was not a pitch. Stinson had explained the Drexciyans so the artwork could be made. They were fighting, he told her, for peace and for the good things in the world. The music, he said, came out of his stomach and his mind when he went into the studio, and it had to come out. In the Warner interview, transcribed by Barnes, he describes the making in one sentence that is also a studio report. It came out of him and out of his partner, from the soul, and they dug it out in one take.

The same interview is where he refuses the job of explaining the Storm Series, the run of records under several names in 2001 and 2002 that Cardew says even specialists cannot agree how to count. “It’s not for me to say, it’s up to the listener. It’s the same way it’s been from day one with all the Drexciya music.” If a listener does not see what he would have named, the naming would be a mistake. “You listen to it, you tell me: what do you feel?” Pressed for the theme, he answered, “An infinite journey through inner space, the space within and find the beauty inside.” Barnes records a separate refusal from Donald. In a 2013 Electronic Beats interview, asked about afrofuturism, Donald said, “I do not wish to specify any particular ethnicity. I would state that all variations of humanity have contributed to the evolution of electronic music.” Barnes notes that neither of them publicly commented on the Quest myth itself. The sleeve had already asked the questions. The producers did not close them.

A compilation that gathers five years of 12-inches and prints a map is doing two kinds of publishing at once. The tracks are the work that already existed, priced, for a while, as imports a lot of listeners could not buy. Cardew’s point about the CD is practical: the music became something you could take home. The notes are a different object, bound to that home copy. They ask a question about the Middle Passage and then say the question will not be answered. The maps beside the question are not mystical. They are a sequence with years on it, and one of the years is the year a music leaves Detroit. The etch on the first EP had already said the techno was from the deep. The 1997 sleeve asked who lived there, set the ships at the beginning of the route, and left the last map blank except for a direction. Stinson died on 3 September 2002. The questions on the sleeve were already written in the form of questions. Reprinting the records did not turn them into answers. The listener was the person he had assigned to finish the job, and he had said so into a microphone in Detroit, the same year, before the year was over.
