A double album on 23 June

New Forms is the debut studio album by Roni Size & Reprazent, released 23 June 1997 on Talkin’ Loud. Later pressings came through Mercury and Universal. The length is 139 minutes and 54 seconds. The producers are Roni Size, DJ Die, Krust, and DJ Suv. It is a drum and bass album on the page’s genre line, and a double album by the clock. Two hours and twenty minutes is not a casual debut. It is a group spending its first album as if the format might not be offered again. The lead single, “Share the Fall,” had come out on 19 May and is filed in the May volume, with the 1996 EP Reasons for Sharing noted there. Three songs from that EP — “Share the Fall,” “Down,” and “Trust Me” — are on the album. The album is not the EP expanded by a few minutes. It is a two-hour object that includes some of the EP.

The UK albums chart position is number 8, over 46 weeks. That is a long stay for a debut that long. People did not buy it once in June and file it. It remained a chart object into other seasons. “Share the Fall” reached 37. “Heroes,” 11 September 1997, reached 31. “Brown Paper Bag,” 27 October 1997, reached 20, the group’s highest single. “Watching Windows,” 28 April 1998, reached 28. The singles keep coming after the album, which means June is the album’s month and not the end of the campaign. Brown Paper Bag in October is the song that did best as a single, after the album had already peaked. The famous excerpt and the album date are not the same event. A magazine that only remembers the October single has lost the debut’s month.

The prize and the certification

The page says the album won the 1997 Mercury Prize, was certified platinum in the UK, and is often called the group’s major work. The prize is an award given to an album in a year, and this piece will not pin the ceremony to a day it has not copied. Platinum is a sales threshold. “Often called” is other people’s language, kept at that distance. What the award does, as a fact, is attach a national album prize to a drum and bass debut in the year of its release. The genre had spent the early 1990s on specialist labels and pirate infrastructure. Talkin’ Loud is not a pirate station. It is a label that could take a two-hour jungle-derived album into the albums chart at number 8 and into a prize shortlist that ended with a win. The infrastructure changed, or at least this record found a door in it. The music’s relationships to the earlier labels — V, Full Cycle, the Bristol cohort around Size — are part of the common telling and are not itemised as dates on the page this piece is using. They are not invented here to thicken the scene.

Four producers are credited, not one auteur with a group name. Size, Die, Krust, and Suv are the line. A debut that long, made by four people, is a collective production even when one name leads the billing. Reprazent is the group name on the sleeve. The producer credit is where the collective is itemised. Instrumental culture’s interest in drum and bass often detaches the break from the song. On a record with this many producers, the break is already a collaboration before any vocalist arrives. The page does not invite a fantasy of a single pair of hands. It prints four.

Length as a position

139 minutes means a listener cannot mistake the album for a singles compilation unless they are being careless. There is simply too much of it. The singles are inside it and also outside it, on their own dates, with their own peaks. The album is the version that asks for an evening rather than a radio slot. Platinum and a number 8 peak say the evening found an audience. Number 37 on the first single says the audience was not automatic. The Mercury Prize says an industry panel, that year, was willing to hear drum and bass as an album genre rather than as a 12-inch genre only. Each of those facts can be argued with. None of them requires a description of a night in a club this desk did not attend.

June 1997 is also the month XL released the Prodigy’s The Fat of the Land, on the 30th. Two British dance albums, seven days apart, different labels, different genres on the tin: drum and bass at Talkin’ Loud, the Prodigy’s big-beat and electronic album at XL. They topped different kinds of attention. Fat of the Land topped the UK albums chart and the Billboard 200. New Forms peaked at 8 and won the Mercury. A month can hold a number-one pop-dance album and a prize-winning double drum-and-bass album without forcing them into a battle. The battle is a later television idea. The calendar only shows that the shops had both.

The useful remainder is the order of operations. EP in 1996. Single on 19 May 1997. Album on 23 June. Further singles in September, October, and the following April. Prize in the 1997 awards year. Platinum as a certification, undated on the page in front of this desk, so undated here. Four producers. Two hours and twenty minutes. Talkin’ Loud. A debut that does not need to be called the moment jungle became respectable, because “respectable” is a compliment with someone else’s manners in it. It needs to be called a double album that charted, won a prize, and kept releasing singles after its month was over.
