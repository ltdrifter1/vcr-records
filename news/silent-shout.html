Silent Shout

The Knife’s third album, released by Rabid in Sweden on 17 February 2006, pitch-shifts a voice until dread becomes a dance record.

Darker than the last one

Silent Shout is the third studio album by the Knife, the Swedish duo Karin Dreijer and Olof Dreijer. Rabid Records, their own label, released it in Sweden on 17 February 2006. Britain received it on 20 March through Brille. They recorded it in Stockholm between March 2004 and November 2005. It runs 48 minutes and 36 seconds. It is synth-pop and techno, and it is darker than Deep Cuts, the 2003 album that had carried Heartbeats into other people’s mouths. The darkness is the subject and the method. A voice is moved off its natural pitch until the person singing becomes a character, a mask, a weather system.

Pitchfork’s Mark Pytlik heard Karin’s performance as a shrill line running through Björk, Ari Up, Siouxsie Sioux, and further out. The Austin Chronicle heard Siouxsie meeting Aphex Twin. The French magazine Magic heard Jay-Jay Johanson meeting Siouxsie. The comparisons agree on one fact: this is pop singing treated as electronic material. The vocals are multi-tracked and pitch-shifted. The result is chilly and physical at once. You can dance to it. You would not call the feeling casual. The visuals match. The title-track video, some of the press photos, and the sleeve take from Oskar Fischinger’s animations and from Charles Burns’ comic Black Hole, a body-horror suburb rendered in black and white. Johan Toorell did the artwork. The duo recorded, programmed, produced, and mixed, with Christoffer Berg and Pelle Gunnerfeldt sharing the mixes and Henrik Jonsson mastering.

A mask you can move to

The title track opens the album at 4:53, a pulse and a voice that seems to have been lowered into another body. It was released as a single on 20 February 2006, three days after the album in Sweden. Neverland and The Captain follow, the latter stretching past six minutes. We Share Our Mothers’ Health, a single in May 2006, is the bodily one, rhythm forward, the lyric a strange inheritance. Na Na Na is the short shock, 2:27. Marble House, out on 1 March 2006, is the duet, with Jay-Jay Johanson credited on the lyric and present as a voice. Like a Pen, 16 October 2006, runs 6:13 and keeps the album’s rule: melody yes, comfort no. Forest Families, One Hit, and Still Light let the record end without a victory lap. Still Light is quiet. The dread does not get a punchline.

Metacritic gathered 22 reviews and arrived at 74. Pitchfork’s own score was 8.6, and the site named the album the best of 2006, with the title track second among the year’s songs. Later it placed the album at number 15 of the decade’s top 200. Resident Advisor had it second for the year and ninth for the decade. The Wire’s critics put it in their 2006 poll. Sweden put the album at number one. The British independent chart put it at 28. The US dance chart put it at 12. By January 2013 Nielsen SoundScan had it at 65,000 copies in the United States. In October 2011 IMPALA certified it gold for more than 75,000 copies across Europe. None of those numbers describes a blockbuster. All of them describe a record that serious listeners refused to treat as a cult footnote.

What Silent Shout changed is the permission it gave to voices. A duo from Stockholm, on their own label, showed that pitch-shifting a singer could be a composition rather than a novelty, and that techno could carry dread without turning into industrial pastiche. A deluxe edition in July 2007 added the Gothenburg concert of 12 April 2006 and the videos. The album itself remains the object. Five years from now the mask will still fit, because the mask was never about a news cycle. It was about a family making electronic music in which the familiar voice is the uncanny one. Play the title track loud enough that the pulse is a fact, then play Still Light. The distance between them is the record. Cold, exact, and, in its own register, generous.
