Walking Wounded

Everything but the Girl’s ninth album, released 6 May 1996 on Virgin, set Tracey Thorn’s voice on drum-and-bass rhythms without turning her into a guest on someone else’s 12-inch.

After Missing

Walking Wounded is the ninth studio album by Everything but the Girl, Tracey Thorn and Ben Watt. Virgin released it in Europe on 6 May 1996. Atlantic released it in the United States on 21 May. It runs 52 minutes and 18 seconds. They recorded it in 1995 at Little Joey’s, Milo, and Strongroom in London. The producers, beside Watt, include Spring Heel Jack, Howie B, Todd Terry, and Rob Haigh, which is a map of the change. Spring Heel Jack and Haigh come from the jungle and ambient-techno side. Howie B comes from the trip-hop side. Terry is the house producer whose remix of Missing, from the previous album Amplified Heart, had turned a sophisti-pop duo into a dancefloor fact. Walking Wounded is the album that takes that fact seriously and writes a whole record inside it.

The genres that attach to it are downtempo, drum and bass, sophisti-pop, techno, and trip-hop. The blend is the sound. Thorn sings as she has always sung, close, conversational, emotionally exact, a little dry, never belting for the sake of the size of the room. Under her, the rhythms are broken. Amen-style breaks, house pulses, bass that moves at a club tempo while the lyric stays at a kitchen-table tempo. The title track is the demonstration: drum and bass at the length of a song, her voice unhurried on top of a rhythm that is in a hurry. Wrong, the next single, is colder. Single and Before Today carry the set further out. The point is not that a folk-adjacent singer discovered a breakbeat. The point is that the songs survive the tempo. The feeling stays legible.

A voice at that speed

The singles plot the year. Walking Wounded came out on 8 April 1996, just before the album. Wrong on 17 June. Single on 23 September. Before Today on 17 February 1997. Britain put the album at number 4, then the duo’s highest albums-chart place. The Billboard 200 put it at number 37. By February 1997 Billboard had worldwide sales at 750,000. The total later passed 1.3 million. Those are not jungle numbers. They are pop numbers for a record that uses jungle’s rhythmic language. The crossover is in the singing and in the decision to keep writing songs, with verses and feelings, instead of handing Thorn to a producer as a hook.

What the album changed is the dignity of that crossover. The mid-1990s were full of dance remixes that improved a song’s weekend and left the artist looking borrowed. Walking Wounded is authored. Watt’s name is on the production. Thorn’s voice is the lead instrument, not a texture. The outside producers are collaborators with a brief, which you can hear: Spring Heel Jack’s influence in the breaks, Terry’s in the house sense of a vocal, Howie B in the weight of the low end. The duo did not vanish into the scene that had remixed them. They used the scene’s tools and remained themselves. British drum and bass gained a singer who was not pretending to be a ragga MC. Adult pop gained a rhythm section that could still fill a club.

Five years from now the title track will still be the cleanest proof that a fast break and a quiet voice can share a song. Play it loud enough that the drums are a physical fact and quiet enough that you can hear her consonants. Then play the album, which is the argument: eleven songs, London, 1995 into May 1996, a duo who had already had the hit and chose to learn the rhythm rather than rent it. The wound in the title is emotional. The walking is the tempo. Both are still intact.
