Ray of Light

Madonna’s seventh album, released 22 February 1998 on Maverick, put William Orbit’s electronics under a voice that had decided to sound like an adult.

After the other songs

Ray of Light is Madonna’s seventh studio album, released by Maverick and Warner Bros. on 22 February 1998. It runs 66 minutes and 52 seconds. She recorded it from June to November 1997 at Larrabee North in North Hollywood and at the Hit Factory in New York, with William Orbit, Patrick Leonard, and Marius de Vries. The music is electronica, trip-hop, techno-pop, and new age, with ambient, house, and strings threaded through. It is the album on which a pop singer at the end of her thirties stopped competing with the teenage market on its own terms and built a different machine.

The path to Orbit was a series of refusals. After Lourdes was born, and after the vocal training for Evita, Madonna wrote with Babyface and then set those songs aside. They sounded, he later said, too close to a ballad she had already sung. Sessions with Rick Nowels produced The Power of Good-Bye, To Have and Not to Hold, and Little Star, but not the electronic direction. Leonard, her great 1980s collaborator, wrote with her and was kept away from the production she did not want, a Peter Gabriel grandeur. Guy Oseary called Orbit. Madonna already knew his Strange Cargo records and his remixes of her own songs. She wanted something futuristic that also sounded old, with Indian and Moroccan colors in the harmony. Orbit brought fragments, eight and sixteen bars. She wrote to them at home and on the road. The computers broke. Recording waited. He played guitar until his fingers bled. She told him not to gild the lily.

Liquid, and a low voice

The sound is Orbit’s liquid electronics under a voice that sits lower and steadier than the voice on her 1980s hits. Frozen, released as a single on 23 January 1998, is the dramatic door: strings, space, a vocal that withholds. The title track, out on 27 April 1998, is the opposite temperature, a rush of breaks and light, euphoria with a spiritual lyric rather than a club’s wink. Drowned World/Substitute for Love opens the album at a whisper. Shanti/Ashtangi sets a Sanskrit shloka to the same production world; she corrected her pronunciation with the scholar Vagish Shastri after the BBC noticed the errors. The Power of Good-Bye, Little Star, and Nothing Really Matters carry the set out through the rest of 1998 and into February 1999. Nothing on the album sounds like a compromise with the sessions she abandoned. The electronics are the songs.

She was studying Kabbalah, Hinduism, and Buddhism, and she was practicing Ashtanga. The lyrics say so without turning the record into a pamphlet. The comfort she described, truths that connect those practices, is audible as tone: wonder, grief, a mother speaking, a woman who had done the blonde pop version and wanted the other piece of her voice. Critics heard it as a magnum opus, and the claim has held. The album is often where people who do not otherwise care for her catalogue agree to stay. The agreement is about the production as much as the celebrity. Orbit’s textures still sound like weather. The breaks still rush. The ballads still have air in them.

It entered the Billboard 200 at number 2, with the biggest first week for a woman at the time, and it reached number one in seventeen countries, Britain among them, along with Australia, Canada, Germany, Italy, and Spain. Frozen and Ray of Light were international top-five hits. Worldwide sales passed sixteen million. At the 41st Grammys it won four awards from six nominations. What it changed is the center of pop’s relationship to electronica. A mainstream singer did not borrow a dance remix as a bonus. She made the electronic arrangement the album. Five years from now the spiritual searching will sound like 1998, because every era’s search has a date. The music has not expired. Put the title track on in a room with a decent low end. The rush is still a rush. Put Frozen on after it. The space is still the point. An adult voice, a producer’s liquid machines, a February release that taught pop how to be electronic without asking permission from the clubs it came from.
