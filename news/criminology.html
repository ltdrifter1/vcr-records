A scene, not a slogan

Criminology is a single by Raekwon, with Ghostface Killah, released on 26 June 1995. It belongs to Only Built 4 Cuban Linx…, Raekwon’s debut album, which Wu-Tang Records, Loud, and RCA put out on 1 August 1995. The RZA produced the album in his basement studio. The single is the scene that travelled first: two voices trading, a crime-film narrative, a soul sample under a drum that knocks, slang dense enough that a casual listener catches the weather before the plot. Raekwon takes the name Lex Diamonds. Ghostface takes Tony Starks. The aliases are part of the composition. You are not hearing a diary. You are hearing characters who have decided the diary would be too small.

The album those characters live in debuted at number 4 on the Billboard 200 and at number 2 on the Top R&B/Hip-Hop Albums chart, with about 130,000 copies in the first week. The RIAA certified it gold on 2 October 1995 and platinum on 24 February 2020. Britain put it at number 85. Heaven & Hell, an earlier single, had come out on 24 October 1994. Ice Cream, the other famous extract, belongs to 1995 without a finer public month. Nas appears on the album, the first guest from outside the Wu-Tang clan, which tells you the doors and the limits. Criminology itself is the internal one. Raekwon and Ghostface, the RZA’s beat, no need for a visitor.

Soul, chopped into a plot

The sound is the RZA’s mid-1990s method at a high concentration. A soul record is chopped until a chord and a sigh become a loop. The drums are dry and hard. The bass is felt in the chest and rarely shows off. There is space in the mix of a kind that later, louder rap would fill with synths. Into that space the two rappers pour detail: names, sums, streets, a cinematic sense of who enters the room. Ghostface’s voice is the more explosive, images tumbling. Raekwon’s is the strategist, calmer, more architectural. The chemistry is the single’s reason for existing. A hook, such as it is, is a mood. You remember the tone of the conspiracy more than a melody you could whistle. That is a crime-film credit sequence translated into a rap record, and it is why the track still starts arguments about the best Wu-Tang solo song.

What Criminology changed, as a June single, was the publicity of a world that the August album would then make unavoidable. Cuban Linx is a debut that behaves like a third act, full of alliances and debts. The single is one act of that play, issued five weeks early so the voices could be in the air. It also fixed Ghostface as Raekwon’s essential counterpart. Later albums by both men are in conversation with this pairing. The purple cassette, the chef, the slang: the mythology is downstream of the music. The music is a beat and two rappers who trusted the listener to keep up.

Hear it beside the rest of the Wu-Tang solo run and the pairing is the distinction. Other members built albums around a single persona. Cuban Linx is a double act inside one name on the sleeve. Criminology is where that double act is easiest to hear, because nothing else is in the way: no posse cut, no comic skit, a beat and two men who know the plot.

Five years from now the slang will still ask you to lean in, which is a gift, not a barrier. Play Criminology loud and the soul loop will still sound like a room with the lights low. Then remember the date of the album, 1 August 1995, and the date of the single, 26 June, and hear the single as a door rather than as a summary. The basement studio, the RZA’s chop, Lex Diamonds, Tony Starks, a first week of 130,000, a gold plaque by October. The criminology of the title is the study. The record is the case.
