They Put the Verse in Court

In September 2026 the Met defended drill lyrics as evidence. A report counted 252 defendants in three years.

format
Essay
volume
9
date
Sep 2, 2026
author
CJ Archer
words
740 words · 3 min
photo
Photo: Joe Gratz

A verse is a made thing. It can describe a crime, invent one, boast about one, quote a film about one, or do all of those in a couplet built to sound harder than the writer’s week. In September 2026 the Metropolitan Police defended the use of drill videos and lyrics as evidence in murder trials, after the campaign group Art Not Evidence asked for the law to change. A report cited in that fight found lyrics used against 252 defendants over three years. That is not an anecdote. It is a method.

The police argument, as detectives have put it in these cases, is that young people sometimes put real information about real violence into songs, because status in the scene rewards it, and that investigators would be negligent to ignore what is said in public. A mother whose son was killed, and whose case involved drill lyrics, has said the same from the other side of grief: if it is acceptable to write about a death, it is acceptable to use the writing to try to get justice. That position deserves to be stated without a sneer. Grief is not a panel discussion. Anyone writing about this from a magazine desk should feel how cheap a free-speech slogan sounds next to a parent.

Art Not Evidence’s argument is the one the law has to answer anyway. Courts have treated the aesthetics of a genre — a mask, a slang term, a beat, a reference to a postcode — as if they were admissions. Drill’s conventions include first person, named enemies, and a theatrical relationship to violence that the music itself often marks as performance and that a jury may not know how to mark. The scrutiny falls overwhelmingly on young Black men. Artists edit themselves because a bar might be printed in a bundle and read flat. Self-editing of that kind is a cultural effect you can predict without a theory degree. If the state builds a pipeline from a verse to a charge, the verse will get vaguer, not because the city got safer, but because the artists noticed the pipeline.

The hard case is the one both sides would prefer not to be the only case. Sometimes a lyric does contain a fact that is also evidence: a detail that was not public, a weapon, a time. A rule that said “never admit a lyric” would protect boasts and also protect a confession that happened to rhyme. A rule that says “always admit a lyric” treats a genre as a diary. The adult position is the annoying one. Lyrics should clear a high bar before a jury hears them. The bar should include context about how the genre speaks, expert evidence that is not just a police officer’s familiarity, and a distinction between a conventional boast and a specific claim. A bill before Parliament, in the period this story was reported, tried to move toward clearer rules. The government’s response was caution. Caution, in this area, usually means the current habit continues.

This is not only a British problem and not only a drill problem. American courts have admitted rap lyrics for decades, and the criticism has been public for decades: the form is read literally when other violent fictions, from country songs to horror films, are read as fiction. The difference is race and class and the usefulness of the lyrics to a prosecution that wants a narrative. Instrumental hip-hop’s popularity has many causes. One of them, for some artists, is the experience of language being used against the speaker. That is a bad reason for a culture to lose its words. It is an intelligible one.

A music magazine’s job here is not to pick a verdict in a case it did not sit. It is to refuse the collapse of a verse into a statement. 252 defendants in three years is the scale of the collapse. The Met’s September 2026 defense is the institution saying the collapse is a method. Art Not Evidence is the counter-institution. Between them is a jury, a parent, and a writer who may have been performing. The performance is not a get-out-of-jail card. It is a fact about how the art works. Courts that cannot be bothered to learn the fact will keep trying songs as if they were statements to the police. They are not statements to the police. They are songs, including when they are dangerous.

CJ Archer
