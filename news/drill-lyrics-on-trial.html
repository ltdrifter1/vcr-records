Bars On Trial

The Met says drill lyrics prove crimes. Not everyone agrees.

Label noteSep 2, 2026

CJ Archer

The Met says drill lyrics prove crimes. Not everyone agrees.

London's Metropolitan Police came out this week defending the practice of using drill videos and lyrics as evidence in murder trials, after a campaign group called Art Not Evidence pushed for a change in the law. A recent report found lyrics had been used as evidence against 252 defendants over a three-year stretch. That's not a rare exception. That's a pattern.

The police argument is straightforward: if someone puts real information about a real crime into a song, that's fair game. A detective who testifies in these cases said young people sometimes reveal true things in music to build status, and that the force isn't trying to demonise a genre, just use what's already there. A mother whose son was killed, and whose case involved drill lyrics as evidence, agreed — if it's fine to write about someone's death, she said, it should be fine to use that writing to help get justice.

Art Not Evidence sees it differently. Their argument is that courts keep treating "gang aesthetics" — a look, a slang term, a beat — as if it's a confession, when it's usually just genre convention, the same way a drill record borrows tropes the way a horror film borrows jump scares. They point out this scrutiny lands almost entirely on young Black men, and that it creates a chilling effect where artists start editing their own art out of fear a bar could end up in a courtroom.

There's a bill in front of Parliament right now trying to set clearer rules for when creative work can be used this way. The government's response so far has been caution rather than commitment — nobody wants to be the one who let a guilty verdict slip through on a technicality, but nobody's found a clean way to separate storytelling from confession either.

This isn't just a UK problem or just a drill problem. Rap lyrics have been read as literal testimony in courtrooms on both sides of the Atlantic for decades. Instrumental hip-hop exists partly because some artists just got tired of their words being used against them. Worth remembering next time a verse gets treated as a police report instead of a record.
