The Cold Vein

Cannibal Ox’s debut, produced by El-P and released on Definitive Jux on 15 May 2001, is an hour of airless, brilliant machinery under two Harlem voices.

A label’s first full length

The Cold Vein is the debut album by Cannibal Ox, Vast Aire and Vordul Mega, produced by El-P and released by Definitive Jux on 15 May 2001. It runs 73 minutes and 45 seconds. It is the label’s first full-length. The path to it was already public. In late 2000 a split double vinyl put three new Company Flow songs beside Iron Galaxy and Straight Off the D.I.C., the two Cannibal Ox pieces that announced the temperature. Those two also appeared on Def Jux Presents, released 20 March 2001. Vein, a single, came in April 2001 with A B-Boy’s Alpha on the flip. The F-Word followed in September 2001. Oxtrumentals, the beats without the voices, came on 19 March 2002. The album is the object all of those satellites orbit.

El-P, who had been one-third of Company Flow, built the tracks. They do not behave like sample-loop beds waiting for a verse. They behave like machines with weather in them: thick synths, drums that sound machined and then scuffed, bass that occupies the body, sudden spaces that feel like a tunnel opening onto a worse tunnel. Reviewers reaching for a comparison reached for science fiction and for a certain kind of instrumental score, Brian Eno’s colder rooms rather than his pretty ones. The comparison is fair as a temperature reading and unfair as a lineage. This is hip-hop. The kick is a kick. The difference is the air. There is very little of it.

Harlem, over the machinery

Vast Aire and Vordul Mega rap like men who can see the machinery and are not impressed into silence. Vast’s voice is round, imagistic, capable of a line that sounds like a comic panel and a line that sounds like a street report in the same breath. Vordul’s is tighter, more clipped, a counter-rhythm. They are from Harlem. A tourist’s Manhattan is absent. What you get are interiors, beefs, metaphysics, and jokes that land sideways. Iron Galaxy, already known from the split, is the overture that stayed: a beat like a factory and a lyric about a planet that happens to be a neighborhood. Straight Off the D.I.C. is the other early chapter. Vein itself is the title’s promise, a track that feels medically cold and then human. The F-Word is the single that let a hook exist without warming the room. Battle for Asgard, Ridiculoid, and The F-Word’s neighbors keep the hour from becoming one idea repeated. There is variety inside the climate. There is no sunshine inserted to help a programmer.

What the record changed is Definitive Jux, and then a decade of underground rap that wanted to sound like pressure. Before The Cold Vein, the label was a plan and a few 12-inches, Company Flow’s afterlife included. After it, Def Jux was a sound people could point at: dense, independent, hostile to radio’s idea of a chorus, in love with rhythm anyway. El-P’s later solo records and his later productions are in this album’s debt, whether they lighten the palette or double down. Cannibal Ox did not become a franchise. The debut remains the statement. That is a kind of luck for the listener. You do not have to sort a long decline. You have one long, finished thing.

The Oxtrumentals disc is the proof that the beats were compositions. Heard alone they still suggest rooms, pursuits, and a city grid under bad weather. Heard with the voices they become songs, because Vast and Vordul treat the density as a collaborator. They do not fight the mix for space they cannot have. They occupy the pockets El-P left, and the pockets are exact. A lesser pairing would have shouted. These two narrate.

Five years from now the machinery will still feel modern, which is the eerie part. Production this heavy usually dates by its plugins. The Cold Vein dates by its intention, and the intention has not gone out of style: make the beat a world, then write like you live there. Play Iron Galaxy first if you need the door. Play the album in order if you want the vein. Definitive Jux, 15 May 2001, 73 minutes and 45 seconds, Harlem voices, a producer who had already decided that independent hip-hop could be enormous. The cold in the title is not a gimmick. It is the mix.
