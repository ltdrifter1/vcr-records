The Cold Vein

Cannibal Ox released the album on 15 May 2001. El-P produced it. It was the first full-length on Definitive Jux. An instrumental companion followed on 19 March 2002.

The first full-length

The Cold Vein is the debut album by Cannibal Ox, produced by El-P and released on his label Definitive Jux on 15 May 2001. It runs 73 minutes and 45 seconds. Wikipedia calls it the first full-length on the label. A reissue later appeared through iHipHop Distribution, dated 2013 on the label line. The 2001 object is the one this volume files. The label was new. The album was how the label became a catalogue rather than a plan. El-P had left Company Flow, whose own debut, Funcrusher Plus, this magazine files in July because that is the month Rawkus released it, in 1997. The path from a Rawkus album to a self-founded label is four years long. May 2001 is the date the new label put a full-length in front of people.

The release was preceded by paper. In late 2000 Definitive Jux issued a split double vinyl: three new Company Flow songs, and two songs from this album, “Iron Galaxy” and “Straight Off the D.I.C.” Those two also appeared on the compilation Def Jux Presents, 20 March 2001. The first single, “Vein,” came in April 2001, with “A B-Boy’s Alpha” on the other side. “The F-Word” followed in September. By 15 May, anyone close to the label had already heard pieces of the album on a split and on a compilation. The street date is not the first time the music left the studio. It is the first time the album, as an album, was the object for sale. That distinction is the same one this run keeps making for Blue Lines, for Illmatic, for The Infamous. Singles and splits are their own dates. The album gets the month of the album.

The instrumental is a second release

On 19 March 2002 Definitive Jux released El-P Presents Cannibal Oxtrumentals. AllMusic’s Victor W. Valdivia, quoted on the Wikipedia page, heard the instrumental set as a soundtrack for an unmade film, closer to Eno’s 1970s work than to a rap album with the voice muted. The comparison is his. The fact that belongs to the magazine is the date and the title. The instrumental record is not The Cold Vein. It is a later object, produced as a presentation of the beats, with El-P’s name in the presenting position. Instrumental hip-hop’s habit of issuing the beats after the vocal album is documented here with two calendar entries ten months apart. May 2001, the songs. March 2002, the beats as their own sequence. Anyone who teaches the music as “just beats” is starting from the second object and calling it the first.

Seventy-four minutes is a long debut. It asks for the patience the short debuts of April — Blue Lines, Illmatic — declined to ask. Length is not seriousness. It is a choice about how many ideas a first album is willing to leave in. El-P’s production on a record this long, for a duo, on a label’s first full-length, is a statement that the label will spend its opening on density rather than on a tight introduction. The split vinyl of late 2000 had already introduced two songs. The album did not have to be short in order to be legible. It could be the long form, because the short form had been handled.

Company Flow’s name on the split is the bridge. The new label did not pretend the previous group was over as a source of recordings. It put new Company Flow songs on the same vinyl as the new album’s songs, then released the new album as the label’s first full-length, then a year later released the instrumentals under El-P’s name as presenter. The authorship is layered on purpose. Cannibal Ox are the artists of the May album. El-P is the producer and the label. The March 2002 record moves his name to the front because the voices are gone and the presenting is the remaining credit. A clean discography keeps all three layers. A messy one says “El-P’s album” and loses the duo, or says “an instrumental album” and loses the fact that the instrumental came second.

What May can hold

May 2001 is a specific week in a specific year, and this volume also holds a 1997 single and a 1994 single and a 2026 album, because months repeat. The Cold Vein does not gain anything from those neighbours except a shelf. Its own neighbours are the March compilation, the April single, and the instrumental LP the next March. That local chronology is tighter than a theme piece about “independent rap” would be. Independent, here, can be replaced with a label name and a first full-length. Definitive Jux, 15 May 2001, 73:45, produced by El-P, instrumental companion 19 March 2002. The sentences are plain because the record’s importance, if it has any to a later reader, will have to survive plain sentences. Ornate ones are where invented nights hide.

The public sequence, and nothing past it. Late 2000, a split double vinyl: three new Company Flow songs, plus “Iron Galaxy” and “Straight Off the D.I.C.” 20 March 2001, Def Jux Presents, those two songs again. April 2001, “Vein,” with “A B-Boy’s Alpha” on the other side. 15 May 2001, the album, the first full-length. September 2001, “The F-Word.” 19 March 2002, the instrumental record, El-P’s name moved to the presenting credit. 2013, an iHipHop reissue, a different object. Funcrusher Plus, 22 July 1997, sits four years earlier and is filed under July. Before May, the label is sending songs out on a split and a compilation. On 15 May it has a 73-minute album. The next March it has the beats as their own release. That is the whole sequence this page will print.
