Madvillainy

Madvillain, MF Doom and Madlib, released the album on 23 March 2004 on Stones Throw. An instrumental version exists as its own object. The vocal album is still the one that came out that day.

23 March 2004

Madvillainy is the only studio album by Madvillain, the duo of MF Doom and Madlib. Wikipedia dates it 23 March 2004, on Stones Throw. The length is 46 minutes and 22 seconds. The studios named are Bionic in Los Angeles, the Bomb Shelter in Glendale, and Doom’s Crib in Atlanta. Recording ran from 2002 to 2004. Madlib and Doom are the producers on the album line. The genre list is long — alternative hip-hop, jazz rap, experimental, psychedelic, abstract, lo-fi — which is what a database does when a record will not sit in one bin. The date and the label are stabler than the bins.

Two singles arrived before the album. “Money Folder,” with “America’s Most Blunted,” is 11 November 2003. “All Caps,” with “Curls,” is February 2004. The February single is the month before the album, not this volume’s assignment. The album is March. A single that shares a title with the way the record looks — all capital letters, a design fact as much as a song — came out while the album was still not in shops. The vocal album is what 23 March refers to. The instrumental release is listed on the same page as a separate object, Madvillainy Instrumentals. This piece will not give that companion a date the infobox does not print in the sentence at hand. It will say the companion exists, because the page says it exists, and that its existence matters to anyone who files hip-hop by the beat.

The beat is not a backing track

Instrumental hip-hop, as a culture, is often described as what remains when a rapper is removed. That description is backwards on a record like this. Madlib’s productions are not beds waiting for a voice. They are sequences with their own length, their own samples, their own decisions about when a loop has said enough. Doom’s voice sits in them. The instrumental album is the evidence that the sequences were considered listenable without the voice, not the evidence that the voice was optional on 23 March. The vocal album is the release. The instrumental is the other release. Confusing them is how a scene turns a rap record into a beat tape against the credits.

Stones Throw, in 2004, was a small label. Wikipedia’s release section, citing Pitchfork, says that after two years of the distributor EMI complaining that the label made records it could not sell, EMI could not keep Madvillainy in stock. The album peaked at number 179 on the Billboard 200. The same section says it sold about 150,000 copies and was one of the label’s best sellers, and that the success let Stones Throw open an office in Highland Park, Los Angeles. Number 179 and a stock problem at the distributor are not contradictory. A small label’s biggest record can be invisible on a major chart and still be more records than the warehouse ordered. The office in Highland Park is a consequence the page is willing to state. It is a better detail than a speech about independence.

“Money Folder” peaked at number 66 on the Billboard Hot R&B/Hip-Hop Songs chart. That is the single chart fact the page gives. The album’s moderate commercial success is described as large for the label and ordinary for the industry. Both scales should be kept. A record can be a landmark inside a catalogue and a modest seller in a country. Landmark, used carefully, means later musicians treated it as a reference, and the page’s language is “often” territory that this piece will not inflate into a unanimous vote. What is unanimous in the paperwork is the date, the personnel, the label, the peak, and the approximate sales.

Los Angeles, Glendale, Atlanta

Three studios in three places is a geography of a duo who did not live in one room. Bionic in Los Angeles is the Madlib side of the map. Atlanta is named as Doom’s Crib. Glendale sits between them in the list, close to Los Angeles and not the same address. A collaboration record is sometimes mythologised as two people in one basement. The studio line says otherwise. It says parts were made in more than one city and assembled into a 46-minute album. Forty-six minutes is short beside the double albums of the 1990s jungle records in other volumes of this magazine, and short beside a 70-minute rap album. The length is a decision. Songs end. The next loop starts. The album does not ask for the patience of a double LP. It asks for attention over a single length that a listener can finish.

March 2004 is also the month, on a different page, when Illmatic’s tenth-anniversary reissue happened, 30 March, with a bonus disc. That reissue is not this album. It is a neighbour on the calendar, ten years after Nas’s debut and a week after Madvillainy. A monthly volume can hold both the anniversary machinery of a 1994 classic and a new Stones Throw album without claiming they were in conversation. They were in the same month. Conversation is something a critic adds. The dates do not require it.

The criticism this record supports, from the paperwork alone, is about credit and sequence. Madlib is a producer whose music was released, the same season, as half of a vocal album and as an instrumental object of its own. Doom is a rapper whose performance is the reason the 23 March release is not filed only under beats. Stones Throw is a label that found out a “difficult” record could empty a distributor’s stock. None of those sentences needs a borrowed memory of a listening party. They need the page, the date, and a refusal to turn the instrumental companion into a replacement for the album that actually came out on 23 March.
