Madvillainy

MF DOOM and Madlib’s album, released 23 March 2004 on Stones Throw, is a stack of short, dusty songs in which the villain and the loop are equal authors.

Short on purpose

Madvillainy is the album by Madvillain, the pair of MF DOOM and Madlib, released by Stones Throw on 23 March 2004. It runs 46 minutes and 22 seconds, and it uses that time as a series of short rooms rather than as two or three long displays. They recorded at Bionic in Los Angeles, at the Bomb Shelter in Glendale, and at Doom’s crib in Atlanta. Madlib made the beats, a private language of jazz, soul, film, and library offcuts, chopped until the loop feels found and inevitable. DOOM raps inside the mask, internal rhymes stacked so tightly that a bar can contain a joke, a threat, and a piece of nonsense that turns out to be the point.

The singles arrived as postcards. Money Folder came out on 11 November 2003 and reached number 66 on the Hot R&B/Hip-Hop chart, a modest number for a record that was never chasing that chart. All Caps followed in February 2004, the title a rule and a hook: the villain speaks in capitals, and the beat is a lurch. The album then entered the Billboard 200 at number 179. Stones Throw, operating out of an office in Highland Park, had put out a record that the mainstream chart could barely see and that a generation of listeners would treat as a foundation. Sales settled around 150,000 in the first long chapter of its life, then kept being discovered. The number is the wrong measure. The measure is how often a later underground record is, quietly, this one.

Dust, and a mask

The sound is dry, close, and slightly wrong in the way a great loop is wrong. Madlib’s drums sit a little behind or a little aside. A horn stab lasts two seconds and becomes the song. A voice from a film, a commercial, or a cartoon wanders through and is never explained. There is no attempt to make a radio arrangement with a sung hook in the middle and a beat switch for the third verse. Songs end when the idea ends, sometimes after a minute and a half. The discipline is the luxury. A listener who wants a single to live inside will find All Caps, Figaro, Meat Grinder, Rainbows. A listener who wants the album will notice that the short tracks are the composition. The sequence is a comic book: scenes, not a three-act confession.

DOOM’s voice is low, unhurried, and amused. He had already built the mask on earlier records, the metal face and the villain name, a way of rapping that treats the self as a character. On Madvillainy the character is at ease. The rhymes fold. A food metaphor becomes a crime metaphor becomes a boast about the rhyme itself. He does not announce the cleverness. The cleverness is the rhythm. Madlib, who also appears as Quasimoto elsewhere in his own catalogue, leaves him room and then crowds him, which is the friendship you can hear. Neither man is the featured guest. The beats are not beds. The rhymes are not decorations.

What the record changed is the scale of independent hip-hop’s ambition. A small Los Angeles label, a producer in a room of records, and a New York rapper in a mask made an album that sounded more complete than the major-label event records around it. They did it by refusing length as a proof of seriousness. Forty-six minutes, dozens of ideas, no skit that overstays, no hook that begs. The instrumental versions that followed let the beats stand alone and proved they could. The rhymed version is still the one. The words are too good to file as optional.

Five years from now the dust will still be the point. You cannot update Madvillainy by cleaning the samples. The degradation, the abrupt endings, the voice in the mask, the joke that takes a second pass: that is the music. Play it on a turntable or on a phone. The low end is modest. The detail is not. All Caps still states its rule. Accordion still sounds like a street that should not have an accordion and does. The album still feels like a private language that forgot to stay private. Stones Throw released it on 23 March 2004. The villain and the beatmaker have both become ancestors. The record has not become a museum. It is too funny, and the loops are too alive.
