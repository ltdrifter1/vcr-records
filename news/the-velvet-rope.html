The Velvet Rope is Janet Jackson’s album of 7 October 1997, on Virgin, produced by Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, the Minneapolis pair who had already made Control and Rhythm Nation and janet. with her. She described the rope, in the campaign and in the album’s own framing, as a boundary: the limit of what you will let near you, and the decision to admit some people past it. The songs take that literally enough to be uncomfortable. Rope Burn, Empty, and the spoken passages are part of the record, not decoration around the singles. Got ’til It’s Gone, with Q-Tip, samples Joni Mitchell’s Big Yellow Taxi. Together Again was written for friends who had died of AIDS. I Get Lonely and Go Deep were the other radio doors.

The useful contrast inside her own catalogue is janet., the 1993 album, which had been sensual and extrovert. The Velvet Rope is sensual and shut. A long tracklist, interludes, a whisper used as a policy. Jam and Lewis keep the low end warm enough that the privacy does not become a lecture. You can dance to Go Deep. You cannot pretend the album is only Go Deep. Together Again is the song that proved a grief record could be a house record without lying about either job. The Joni Mitchell sample is the other proof: a singer with her pick of collaborators chose a specific older song and a specific rapper and left both of them audible.

Pop albums of that length often pad. This one uses the length as a house with rooms, some of them locked. The rope in the title is the instruction for how to listen. You are being told who the record is for, and you are being told it is not for everyone in the same way. Virgin released it on 7 October 1997. Jam and Lewis produced it. Together Again is still the one to play when you want the thesis in a single that a room can actually use.
