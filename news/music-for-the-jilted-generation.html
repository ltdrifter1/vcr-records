Music for the Jilted Generation

The Prodigy’s second album, released 4 July 1994 on XL, is 78 minutes of breakbeat rave with the singles already armed and the long tracks stranger than the hits.

Between the debut and the stadium

Music for the Jilted Generation is the second album by the Prodigy, released by XL on 4 July 1994. Liam Howlett made it with Neil McLellan. They recorded through 1993 and 1994 at Strongroom in London and at Earthbound in Braintree, Essex, the same two rooms that would later hold The Fat of the Land. This one runs 78 minutes and 7 seconds. It sits between Experience, the 1992 debut, and the 1997 album that went to number one on both sides of the Atlantic. The job of the second album was to prove the rave act could think in lengths, not only in 12-inches. It does that, and it also contains the most undeniable rave hook the group had yet written.

The singles are a calendar. One Love came out on 4 October 1993, a relatively expansive track, more sunrise than riot. No Good (Start the Dance) came out on 16 May 1994, built on a vocal sample from Kelly Charles’s You’re No Good for Me, the phrase turned into a command a field could shout. Voodoo People followed on 12 September 1994, a guitar figure and a break, a track that feels like a chase. Poison came later, on 6 March 1995, and kept the album in the world after the year had turned. The May single is the popular door. The album is wider than the door. Its outer tracks run long, change section, and behave like pieces of a night rather than like adverts for the singles.

Breaks, and a title that picks a side

The sound is breakbeat techno, rave, and hardcore, played by a producer who had learned how to arrange. Howlett’s sequencers are busy and clear. A break is chopped until it becomes a riff. A synth stab answers it. Bass is rude without being only rude. McLellan’s co-production is in the finished, physical quality of the mixes: they hit, and they also have mids you can hear on a cheap system. No Good is the lesson in economy. The vocal sample is the hook, the break is the engine, and the drop back into the loop is the thrill. You can reduce it to a chant and still feel the record under the chant. Voodoo People is the other anthem, more linear, a riff you could mistake for rock until the rhythm reminds you where you are. Their Law, with Pop Will Eat Itself, is the collaboration that says the group were willing to share a track with a guitar band and still sound like themselves.

The long tracks are the reason it is an album and not a singles file. Break and Enter opens like a raid. The Narcotic Suite is a run of pieces that slow the pulse and then rebuild it, a side of the group the hits do not advertise. Claustrophobic Sting and the tracks around it keep the Essex studio’s hours in the music: this is not a record made to be one mood for four minutes. It is a record made by people who stayed in the room. The title takes a side. A jilted generation, in 1994, meant the criminalisation of rave, the sense that a public music had been told it was a nuisance. The album does not lecture. It dances in a way that makes the lecture unnecessary. The breaks are the politics. They continue.

What the record changed is the Prodigy’s scale and, with it, the idea of a British rave album. Experience had been a collection of the early fire, Charly and Everybody in the Place and Out of Space, a debut that sounded like a pirate station’s greatest night. Jilted Generation is composed. It has a suite. It has a guest. It has a single so obvious that the rest of the album can afford to be odd. When The Fat of the Land arrived three years later, the audience was ready because this record had already moved the group from the rave tape to the album chart’s serious attention. The second album is often where a dance act either repeats or thins out. This one thickened.

Five years from now No Good will still start dances, in rooms that know the sample and in rooms that only know the shout. The album is the context that keeps the shout honest. Play the single for the hook. Play The Narcotic Suite for the part of Howlett that was already thinking in chapters. Play Voodoo People for the chase. XL, 4 July 1994, 78 minutes and 7 seconds, Strongroom and a studio in Braintree, Howlett and McLellan. The generation in the title has aged. The breaks have not softened. That is the review.
