Music for the Jilted Generation

The Prodigy’s second album was released on 4 July 1994 through XL. It runs 78 minutes and 7 seconds. Liam Howlett and Neil McLellan produced it, in London and in Braintree.

4 July 1994

Music for the Jilted Generation is the Prodigy’s second studio album, released 4 July 1994 on XL Recordings. Liam Howlett and Neil McLellan produced it. The studios are Strongroom in London and Earthbound in Braintree, Essex. Recording is dated 1993–1994. The length is 78 minutes and 7 seconds. Experience, the debut, is 1992 in the chronology. The Fat of the Land, the third album, is 30 June 1997, made in the same two studios with Howlett alone on the producer line. The second album is the long one. Seventy-eight minutes is a full CD at a time when a full CD was a statement that the group had more than a run of singles. The singles exist anyway. “One Love” is 4 October 1993. “No Good (Start the Dance)” is 16 May 1994, and it is filed in the May volume. “Voodoo People” is 12 September 1994. “Poison” is 6 March 1995. July is the album. The campaign is longer than July.

The genre list — rave, breakbeat techno, techno, hardcore techno, breakbeat hardcore — is a list of names for music that was changing its name while this record was being made. 1994 is late for “rave” as a newspaper word and early for the album-shaped electronic rock the group would take to number one in 1997. The page uses several of the names at once, which is more honest than a single retro label. A record can be hardcore and breakbeat and still be sequenced as an album with a title about a generation. The title is the group’s. This piece will not paraphrase it into a social history it has not researched beyond the sleeve. Titles are claims. The claim can be quoted by being printed, and left as a claim.

Two rooms

Strongroom and Earthbound are the geography. London and Essex. The number of records in this magazine that pass through Strongroom is already more than one: Underworld’s Dubnobasswithmyheadman, January 1994, names it. A studio is a business with a diary. In the first half of 1994 that diary included at least these two albums that later became part of the British electronic story. Braintree is the other entry, and it keeps the Prodigy from being written as a London group who sometimes mentioned a hometown. The second studio is in the town. The July album is made in both. When the same rooms return for the 1997 album, the continuity is practical. They did not have to invent a new workplace in order to become a chart-topping group. They returned to the workplace that had already held a 78-minute record.

Neil McLellan’s name on the 1994 producer line, and its absence from the 1997 line in the sources used here, is a personnel fact. Howlett is the constant. McLellan is the collaborator on the second album. Credits that change between albums are how a group’s method becomes visible. A single-producer myth is harder to maintain when the previous album printed two names. The Fat of the Land piece in the June volume records Howlett alone because that is what its page says. This piece records two names because this page says two names. The difference is the point.

What 78 minutes is for

A 78-minute album cannot be a singles EP with the artwork enlarged. There has to be material that was not the May single and would not be the September single. The album is the place that material lives. Listeners who know the group from “No Good” or from the later number ones meet a different duration here. Duration is not a virtue by itself. It is evidence of scope. Scope, on a second album, is the decision that the first album’s format — Experience, 1992 — was not the outer limit. XL’s decision to release that scope in July, after a May single, is a label treating hardcore records as albums with campaigns, not as a run of 12-inches only. The 12-inches still came out. They have dates. The album has a different date and a different length.

Essex is easy to sentimentalise, and this page will not. Braintree appears because a studio called Earthbound was there, and the album was made in it. No claim follows about a county, a class, or a night. London appears because Strongroom was there. The useful map is two workplaces and a label in a catalogue number’s absence — this page has not cited an XL catalogue number for the 1994 album, and will not guess one. The 1997 album’s XL121 is on the shop page. The 1994 album, in the sources at hand, is the date, the label, the length, the rooms, and the two producers.

July 1994 also sits a month after Underworld’s “Dark & Long” single and a year and a half after Dubnobasswithmyheadman. Orbital’s Snivilisation follows on 8 August, on FFRR. The summer of 1994, read only as release dates, is a stack of British electronic albums and singles from groups who did not share a label. XL, Junior Boy’s Own, FFRR. The stack is not a movement just because the months touch. It is a season in which album-length electronic records were a normal thing to issue. Normal is a stronger historical claim than revolutionary. Revolutionary is what sleeve notes say. Normal is what a calendar full of release dates shows.

The single in May told people to start the dance. The album in July did not have to repeat the instruction. It had to be long enough that the instruction was not the whole record. Seventy-eight minutes is that length. Howlett and McLellan are the producers of it. Strongroom and Earthbound are where it was made. XL is who released it, on 4 July 1994. The third album, three years later, would top charts the second album did not need in order to be the long hardcore record. This volume’s cover is the long record, not the later coronation.
