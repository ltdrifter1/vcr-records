Gus Lawrence put a spoken-word sample on "Mr Kirk's Nightmare" and did not tell the others until he played them the track. The vocal had been in a pile of records Marc Clair brought round. Marc wanted it off. Gus thought the argument would sell the record. He said this to Paul Terzulli, in an interview Wax Poetics ran as a rediscovery of the track. The 4hero site describes the same record as an Isley Brothers break with a spoken-word sample about an overdose. It sat on Combat Dancin', Reinforced RIVET 1202, a UK 12-inch dated 1990. Discogs times "Mr Kirk's Nightmare" at 6:07 and credits the writing to The Rebel, D. McFarlane, Reinforced Gus and Mark Mac. Reinforced Gus mastered it, at Stone Cold Studios. Damont pressed it.

The name on the credit and the name on the label are the same idea. Gus told Terzulli his production name was Reinforced Gus, and that the label took the name from him. He had a stack of hip-hop instrumentals, about thirty, and he had tried Rhythm King and Jive. Marc, sitting in a college common room, said they needed their own label if the music was going to come out. Talking to Hanna Bächer in 2016, for Red Bull Music Academy, Marc said he had watched what happened when a label put a group on a retainer and then shelved them. His goal was to do it themselves.

The 4hero site puts the start in 1989. Marc grew up in Harlesden, the son of a Windrush-generation Jamaican family. By the mid-1980s he was running sound systems, Solar Zone and Midnight Lovers, and through that he met Denis "Dego" McFarlane. The same year, Marc and Dego set up Strong Island, a pirate station broadcasting from Dollis Hill, and Marc and Gus started Reinforced. Dego and Iain Bardouille joined. Four people, so 4hero. Marc's account of the station, in the 2016 conversation, is two turntables and a Realistic mixer. A friend from college built the transmitters. They carried them up into the blocks and installed them in the lift shafts. The studio was a safe house, and a link ran from there to a block somewhere else, so the aerial and the people were not in the same place. Iain Bardouille said he had been pulled into the sound system with Marc, and that the two of them had the midnight slot on a station called Girls FM. Gus said the jingles were made in his bedroom, with Marc, Iain and Dego coming round to knock something up.

A thousand in a weekend

The first record was not "Mr Kirk's Nightmare". Gus said it was "All B 3", 700 copies, a white label, and that it took about two months to shift, half of them probably given away as promos. The 4hero site calls that first single "All B 3 / Rising Son", 1990. Discogs lists the 12-inch as Reinforced RIVET 1201. Reinforced's own list puts the same number in 1990 and titles it "Rising Sun / All Be Free". Three names, one number. The number is what a shop could reorder.

RIVET 1202 was the second release, the five-track Combat Dancin' EP, with "Mr Kirk's Nightmare" on it. Gus said they had not made the track for clubs or for DJs. They had made it for themselves and for the shops. He remembered thinking Steve Jackson would like it, and that Jackson championed it on Kiss FM. They pressed a thousand. Zoom in Camden took 250. Blackmarket took 250. Two other shops took 250. The thousand went in a weekend. A week later they came back with another thousand, and those went too, out of the back of his mother's Nissan. A shop, he said, would usually take a box of thirty.

Pacific then wanted the track as a proper single. Gus's figures, given to Terzulli, split the pressings: 2,500 copies of RIVET 1202, the EP, and 28,000 of RIVET 1203. The label's own list has RIVET 1203 as "Mr. Kirk's Nightmare", 1990. The single is the one that left the five-track EP behind. Then the distributor went under. In the 2016 conversation Gus remembered a cheque the bank sent back. He thought it was for £23,000. He phoned Nigel at Pacific, who said he would write another. It did not come. Iain said the liquidators' letter told them they might get 2p in the pound. Marc remembered a pressing bill of about £10,000 still to pay. Gus said the bill was more like £15,000, and that Pacific owed them about £33,000, about half of it debt. He had not understood VAT. After the bankruptcy a VAT inspector sat him down with his dad in the front room and told him the office could close them. Then the office let them off.

The way out of the debt, in Gus's telling, was to press more records. He said that by the time they reached record number nine they had paid the plant, and that Manix selling 30,000 copies was the release that actually got them paid. He took £5,000 in five-pound notes to the pressing plant in a black suitcase, wearing dark glasses. "I thought I was the don." The label's list has a Manix 12-inch, "Feel Real Good", at RIVET 1209, in 1991. He did not, in the interview, tie the 30,000 to a catalogue number. The suitcase is the part he was willing to describe.

The last page

In Rough Territory, the first 4hero album, is 1991. Reinforced's album list gives it as Rivet LP 001. Discogs gives the UK vinyl as RIVET LP 01. The 4hero site calls it the only album with all four founders as full members. Gus, asked in 2016 whether 4hero had felt like a band, said that in 1991 the album was a way to sell enough to clear the debt. Someone in the same conversation answered that he had been trying to make the music. The debt and the record are both in the transcript. They are not the same motive.

The machines, as Gus listed them, were an Akai S-950, a Fostex four-track, an SY-22, a Jamo amp, a VST-Creator, an Atari 1040, and the drum machines, an 808, a 909 and a 606. Iain added that soundsystem equipment which should not have been in a studio was in the studio anyway. The move into Dollis Hill, in that conversation, is a loft. Other people in the building had serious gear. They looked into the little room and walked off grinning. The room was for trying things.

Time-stretching, Gus said, was the last page of the S-950 manual. He and Dego sat down and went through the pages, editing and then editing again, and on the last page there was a function they had not used. They tried it. The breaks sounded wrong in a way they wanted. He was careful about the word. Pitch-shifting and time-stretching were, to him, different tricks. The pitch-shift he first heard from Goldie, who phoned at about three in the morning and played "Terminator" down the line, drums run through a device Gus called an H-3000 Harmonizer. They called the settings "the parameters". The time-stretch was the manual. They sampled the same break five or six times at different pitches and kept the length, so the break stayed in time while the sound changed. Gus named the first record they used it on as 4hero's "Journey From The Light". The label's list has that record at RIVET 1235, in 1993. He also thought a Manix track, "The X Factor", had it. He was sure about the 4hero record.

An ideas man, then a double LP

The 4hero site says DJ Kemistry introduced Goldie, that he began by designing artwork for the label, and that he learned production from Marc and Dego before his first records came out as Rufige Kru. Gus, in 2016, described the sessions from the engineering side. Goldie was an ideas man. He would not touch the equipment. Dego and Gus did the engineering and the co-production. Goldie would ask for a break that sounded like a door opening and closing, or for keys that sounded like flowers blooming. Gus called it sound design before they had the phrase for it, and said Goldie's speed pushed the Manix records toward quick changes instead of a groove left to roll. The label's own list puts the early Rufige records in 1992: "Killa Muffin / Krisp Biscuit", RIVET 1220, under the spelling Rufige Cru, and "Darkrider", RIVET 1224, under Rufige Kru. A 1993 line, RIVET 1244, is "Ghosts / Terminator Ii". The phone call and the catalogue are not the same object. One is a track played down a receiver at 3am. The other is a number the label was willing to print.

By 1994 Marc and Dego were the duo. Parallel Universe came out on Reinforced as a double LP, RIVET LP 04. Discogs dates the vinyl to 1994 and the label's own CD, RIVET CD 004, to 1995, alongside a European CD on Selector, SEL 3, and a Japanese CD on Sublime, SBLCD5004. The titles on the vinyl include "Terraforming", "Sounds From The Black Hole", "Power To Move The Stars", "Sunspots" and "Wrinkles In Time". "Universal Love" credits the vocal to Carol Crosby. The 4hero history describes the record as breakbeats used to build atmosphere, not only energy. The sci-fi titles are the sleeve's way of saying the same thing. A 12-inch that sells a thousand in a weekend is a report from the shop. A double LP with a track called "Sounds From The Black Hole" is a bet that someone will stay for four sides.

The label's list starts an Enforcers series at RIVET 1223, in 1992, and keeps numbering it for years. In the 2016 conversation the reason given for leaving the picture-disc EPs off the digital reissues was that the people who had the vinyl should go on holding it. The limited picture discs stayed objects. The rest could be heard by someone who had never been to Dollis Hill. That split is the label's late version of the early problem. Rhythm King and Jive would not put the instrumentals out, so four people in north-west London pressed them. A distributor's cheque came back. A suitcase of fivers went to the plant. An S-950 manual had one page left, and the page changed the length of a break without changing the clock. RIVET 1201 has been called three different titles. RIVET 1203, in Gus's account, ran to 28,000. The number is the part a shop could trust. The last page of the manual is the part a listener can still hear.
