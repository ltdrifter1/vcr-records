Fact, on 19 January 2010, quoted Mark Ernestus from Derek Walmsley's interview in that February's Wire. In the extract, a killer record moves 150 copies. Fact's brackets put the words "we sell" into the sentence, so the insertion stays visible. The next record, Ernestus's phrase, is "total shit," and will not sell three. The seriousness, he said, belongs to the producers and to the audience. It either works or it doesn't. Hard Wax had just marked twenty years. The numbers are the shop's review. They do not come with a paragraph attached.

The shop opened in December 1989, on Reichenberger Straße in Kreuzberg. Ernestus, speaking to Tobias Rapp as the manager and named in the piece as Mark, said there had simply been no record shop in Berlin that took acid and house seriously. One distributor handled electronic dance music, so the maxi-singles people wanted rarely reached Germany. Hard Wax started bringing them in from Detroit, Chicago and New York. Berlin, he told Rapp, had felt like the absolute provinces. Then they travelled to Detroit and Chicago and found there was hardly any communication between the scenes in those cities either. Rapp filed this on 14 November 1996, in German, for taz. The English here is a translation of that piece.

His account of the next few years is a traffic report. By 1992, when Berlin's techno scene blew open, DJs and producers from elsewhere were in and out of Reichenberger Straße. He names Jeff Mills, Carl Craig and Ron Trent among the people who came through the Hard Wax connection, made music in the city, and bought records in the shop. He also writes down the pressure. There were times, Ernestus said, when DJs pushed the counter, especially while Hard Wax was still the only shop, and the staff tried to hold the line. The fanfare end of the big rave was not what they stocked. Rapp says the slow collapse of that movement was watched from behind the counter with something closer to satisfaction than regret.

Twenty-five thousand records, six turntables

By the day Rapp's article ran, the move had already happened, that Monday, to Paul-Lincke-Ufer 44a. About 25,000 records had been packed, carried and filed again. The new shop had six turntables. A customer who wanted one no longer had to get there with his elbows. A sofa corner was planned and not yet built. Ernestus was glad of the neighbours and of the room. Buying records and hanging out, he told Rapp, belong together. At last there was space for both.

Basic Channel's studio was in the same building. Rapp writes that producers could have a dubplate cut there, one plate of a new track, and that the label cared more about the music than about the sales figures. The label is Ernestus and Moritz von Oswald, begun in Berlin in 1993. Dunn Johnson, in XLR8R in July 2014, puts the shop first and the records after it. Ernestus and von Oswald made the dub-techno singles. A label under the same name released their own music. Chain Reaction released other artists. Dubplates & Mastering existed so the cuts could meet the standard the two of them wanted. The Haus der Kulturen der Welt's biography of Ernestus dates that studio to the mid-1990s and describes it as Berlin's first mastering and vinyl-cutting room set up for club music.

Between 1993 and 1994 the label issued nine 12-inches under several names. Rory Gibb, in The Quietus on 14 May 2018, calls Cyrus's "Enforcement" the record that opened the label, and notes a Jeff Mills remix on the B-side. On the discography Wikipedia compiles from the label's pages, that single is BC-01. Basic Channel, "Phylyps Trak," is BC-02. The sequence runs on through Vainqueur's "Lyot RMX," Quadrant's "Q1.1," Cyrus's "Inversion," and Basic Channel's "Quadrant Dub," "Octagon," "Radiance" and "Phylyps Trak II," which is BC-09. Gibb describes the label art as greyscale, a cryptic lock-like mark, and says the nine came in quick succession. BCD, a CD of edits taken from the 12-inches, came out in 1995. BCD-2 followed in 2008. A listener looking for a biography on the sleeve will not find one. Ernestus told Walmsley he would rather be invisible. What he wanted, he said, was his genetic code inside the genetic code of the music.

No divide

Dub was the other half of the shelf, and it was not a hobby kept in the back. At the Red Bull Music Academy in Barcelona in 2008, von Oswald told Torsten Schmidt that he had listened to reggae for years, British imports, Channel One, Lee Perry, records a shop would sit down with because they were hard to get. At the start of the 1990s he went back to that pile with Ernestus, who was, von Oswald said, even deeper in the difficult 7-inches and 12-inches. They fixed on Lloyd Barnes, Wackies, a New York producer born in Jamaica and working in the South Bronx. They were in the city digging cellars and basements anyway, and they met him. A contact in London had the tapes of a Wackies production called "Mango Walk" that had not come out. Von Oswald said they had the original tapes, released the track, and made a remix. He wanted the room to hear the space in the original: room for the voice, the bass, the percussion and the effects, and not too much of any of them. The bass, he said, was sometimes a pulse more than a note you could point to. Wikipedia's discography for the label, drawn from the label's own pages, lists a 1998 12-inch, RS-02, pairing the Chosen Brothers' "Mango Walk" with Rhythm & Sound's "Mango Drive."

Ernestus put the same principle as a test. In an Electronic Beats interview that Johnson quoted in 2014, he said that if you love techno and do not at least appreciate dub, or other music built on reduction and repetition, something is wrong. There is not supposed to be a divide. Johnson, in the shop, found a wall of reggae and dub 45s next to the Basic Channel archive and the Detroit rarities. The section growing fastest was the one they called Outernational, stocked in part with reissues and with records tied to the musicians Ernestus was working with in Senegal. The HKW biography dates Mark Ernestus' Ndagga Rhythm Force to 2011. Johnson also notes the Wackies reissues Hard Wax had been carrying, Horace Andy and Sugar Minott among the names that came back into print and into the bags of people who had come in for house.

The word they took off the list

The short descriptions began as a cost. Michael Hain had been behind the counter since 2001. When Johnson visited he was store manager, and he traced the clipped language to printed catalogues mailed to subscribers. Paper and postage set the length. You wrote the precise thing because there was no room for a second sentence. Mail order was there from the beginning, Hain said, because Ernestus knew how hard the music was to find if you did not live in Berlin, London or Hamburg. Once the website had a shop on it, the rule was that the page and the room held the same records. What you see on the site is what you get in the shop. Hain spoke about customers in small cities from Australia and Taiwan out to Malta and Singapore. You do not, he said, need a Berghain in your town to get the music.

Precision picked up a catchphrase. For a while the strongest mark on a Hard Wax recommendation was one word, TIP!. In 2012 Joy Orbison put the habit in a title, "Big Room Tech House DJ Tool – TIP!". The track was released in 2013 on Nonplus's compilation Think and Change. Johnson writes that the joke travelled far enough for the shop to retire the word. Hain said he had found it funny, and that TIP! had become a thing it was never meant to be. A stamp people can collect is no longer a description of a record. The list went back to describing the record.

Three addresses

On 13 October 2023 the shop posted that it would open on Monday 30 October at Köpenicker Straße 70, 10179 Berlin-Mitte, inside Kraftwerk, with the door between Tresor and Ohm. April Clare Welsh, in DJ Mag three days later, counts 27 years at Paul-Lincke-Ufer. The Kreuzberg building was headed for luxury flats. Dubplates & Mastering, which she describes as 28 years old and housed in the same building, had already said that the new owner terminated the lease after October 2023, and that they were looking for another place. The first address was the street where a shop imported what the distributor would not carry. The second was the canal, six turntables, a studio, a cutting room. The third sits between two clubs. About 25,000 records had moved once already, in 1996. They moved again. The descriptions written for a printed list were still short enough to travel.

Gibb, writing about the early Wire profile, says that in 1995 Ernestus and von Oswald gave Biba Kopf their first names only, and asked that the interview not be taped. Johnson's retelling of the trip to the shop has them playing Kopf three hours of Chicago and then Detroit: Phuture, Armando, Armani, then Cybotron, Model 500, Underground Resistance, Jeff Mills, Robert Hood, Juan Atkins. A surname withheld and a sleeve left nearly blank are the same manners. A counter that will sell 150 of one record and three of the next is publishing a criticism in quantities, every week, without writing the criticism out. Someone in a town with no club can still read the quantities. The building on the canal could be sold out from under the cutting room. The list was made to be carried.
