The bag holds almost nothing on purpose: a lip, a key, and a polite fiction about having somewhere to put the phone, which is actually in the boot of whoever drove, tucked beside the card case you’ll fish out between tracks when the bar finally looks at you. The stall will hold the rest (the coat, the breath, the decision to stay another hour). I watched someone at the cloakroom try to check a bag that wasn’t really a bag, just a studded suggestion of one hanging from a silver chain, and the boy behind the counter looked personally betrayed, as if utility had ever been the point of walking into a room designed to make practicality look like a failure of imagination.

It wasn’t. The mini is a policy dressed as an accessory. I am not carrying your night. The cute sits smaller this time, and meaner about its boundaries. Boys still ask where they put their stuff, as if the answer weren’t already walking past them in four-inch silence, as if “stuff” were the measure of whether they belonged under the strobe.

In 2003 it was a pocket in low-rise everything and a lip gloss that leaked a sticky apology onto whatever it touched, a soft disaster you wiped on the inside of your wrist. In 2014 it was a backpack you swore was ironic while it quietly held everybody’s chargers and became the unofficial lost-and-found. This year the mini is the fit, not a utility argument, not a debate about practicality, just a hard little no you can wear on a chain. The petition class will call it impractical. The night is impractical. That’s why you left the apartment in the first place, why you chose a heel that argues with the sidewalk, why you keep refusing to be the designated pocket for a room full of people who packed like the evening owed them logistics.

Hold their drink if they ask. Don’t ask where the phone is, and don’t make them narrate the logistics of being small on purpose. If they have to go back to the boot between tracks, you wait without performing patience like a favor you can cash later. The xerox of the evening is already on the collarbone (flash, sweat, the soft bruise of a night that didn’t need pockets). The bag was never for stuff. It was for the photograph of not having stuff, for the right to travel light while the rest of the room invents emergencies. Wear it. Or don’t. The stall still has a lock either way, and the lock has always been the real bag, the one place that holds what you actually need until you’re ready to be seen again.
