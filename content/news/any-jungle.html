On 30 August 2026 Night Shift ran a review of a L.T. Drifta record called any jungle. The page described a long piece, a white-vinyl image with Club Copy lettering, and a kind of jungle that “doesn’t treat you like a tempo problem.” The artwork file is still on the site. A ninety-second preview file is in the site’s audio folders, which is the length of a store preview, not evidence of the full duration. What is not on the site is a release page, a price, a catalogue number, or a line on the artist page. That page says six titles. any jungle is not one of them.

This is the same situation as Lions’ Gate, and it wants the same remedy. The URL of the review remains. The claim that the record is part of the catalogue does not. Keeping the old review’s sensory writing — lashes, coats, a clock the writer forgot to look at — would be keeping a listening diary of an object the label is no longer offering. Diaries can be true and still be the wrong text to leave in front of a new reader. The right text is a short accounting.

Jungle, as a word, is otherwise all over L.T. Drifta’s presentation and almost nowhere on his release genres. The artist page tags him “Jungle” and “the soulful side of jungle and hip-hop.” The roster says “Jungle / Breaks.” The six priced records are instrumental hip-hop, jazz, and electronic. So a title as blunt as any jungle would have been, if it had stayed in the list, the one object that used the genre as a name rather than as a bio. Its absence means the genre word is still a bio word. The records you can pay for do not put it on the sticker. The mixes come closer. On the mixes page, several uploads are titled Deep in Tha Jungle and dated, in the titles, to the mid-1990s: 1993–96, 1994 hardcore, 1996 atmosphere. Those are mixes, attributed on the page to L.T. Drifta, not originals for sale. They are also the place where the word “jungle” is doing documentary work — pointing at a period of records — rather than promotional work.

A ninety-second preview without a release is a particular kind of digital leftover. Stores use short previews so you can sample a track you might buy. A preview that outlives the product page is a clip with nowhere to land. Describing the music inside it, in any detail, would be reviewing a sample of an unpublished or withdrawn record. That can be done when an artist asks for it. It should not be done by a label’s magazine in order to fill an old URL. The image alt on the original story — white vinyl, black ground, Club Copy lettering — is a description of a picture, and the picture can stay. Pictures of sleeves for records that did not remain on the list are part of how labels look while they are deciding. They are not the discography.

The discography a stranger should trust is the one the artist page is willing to count. Six. Plus, if they want the listening, forty mixes on the mixes page, including a series called NIGHTSHIFT that this issue writes about separately. any jungle belongs with the drafts, the pulled experiments, and the titles that made sense in a month and less sense in the binder. August 2026 was a busy month on this desk. The note got a headline, a dek, and a mood. The catalogue, by October, had not adopted it. Catalogues are allowed the last word. They are the part a listener can hold the label to.

If the title returns, with a number and a date and a full file, it can be reviewed as music. Until then the sentence is administrative and, for that reason, more durable than the one it replaces. any jungle was written up here on 30 August 2026. It is not among the six titles L.T. Drifta’s Club Copy page lists. The jungle in his public work is in the bio and in the mix titles. The records are elsewhere, and they have numbers.
